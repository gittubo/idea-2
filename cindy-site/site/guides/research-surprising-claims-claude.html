the idea

What this actually does

This turns Claude into a fast research assistant that does four things in order: finds whether a real published study exists, pulls the actual numbers (sample, who was studied, how long), tells you if it is correlation or genuine cause, and names what the study does NOT prove. That last part is the gold, because the viral version almost always overreaches past what the researchers actually found.

One important heads-up: Claude is a starting point, not the final word. It can miss a source or misremember one, so for anything you are about to publicly repeat, click through to the primary study it cites and read the abstract yourself. Treat Claude as the fast first pass that tells you WHERE to look.

The 4-step prompt chain

Run these one after another in the same chat so Claude keeps the context. Swap the example claim for whatever you are checking.

Step 1: does a real study even exist

1 · does the study exist 
Search the web and tell me: is there a real, published, peer-reviewed study behind this claim? "A study tracked 5,000 people for five years and found your partner's personality predicts your salary more than your boss does." Give me the actual study title, the authors, the journal, and the year, with a link. If you cannot find a real source, say so plainly instead of guessing.

Step 2: pull the real numbers

2 · the real numbers 
For that study, tell me the exact sample size, who was actually studied (ages, relationship status, country), and how long it ran. Then compare those real details to how the viral post described it, and flag anything the post got wrong or exaggerated.

Step 3: correlation or cause

3 · correlation or cause 
Does this study show a correlation or did it prove cause and effect? Explain in plain terms which one it is and why that matters for how confidently I can repeat the claim.

Step 4: what it does NOT prove

4 · what it does not prove 
List what this study does NOT prove, and where the viral version overreaches beyond the actual finding. Give me one honest sentence I could post that stays true to what the research really showed.
a worked example

Watch it work on a real claim

The reel's example is a genuinely real study, which is what makes it a perfect teaching case: the study is real, but the viral retelling bends it.

📚 The real study is "The Long Reach of One's Spouse" by Brittany Solomon and Joshua Jackson, published in Psychological Science in 2014 (study page, WashU write-up). It followed nearly 5,000 married people aged 19 to 89 over five years, with both partners working in about 75% of couples.

✅ What it actually found: people with a more conscientious partner reported higher job satisfaction, higher income, and more promotions, even after accounting for their own conscientiousness.

⚠️ Where the viral version overreaches: it is a correlation, not proof of cause. The researchers proposed three theories for WHY (a conscientious partner does more at home, models good habits, and lowers stress), but they call these explanations, not proven mechanisms (Scientific American). So "choose your partner like your salary depends on it" is a fun hook, but the honest version is "people with conscientious partners tended to do a bit better at work over time, and we are not certain exactly why."

3 bonus prompts to run next

🕵️ Spot the funding bias:

1 · spot the funding bias 
Who funded this study, and does the funder have a stake in the result? Flag any conflict of interest I should know before I share it.

📉 Check if it replicated:

2 · did it replicate 
Has this finding been repeated by other independent studies, or is it a single study that never replicated? A one-off result is much weaker than a repeated one.

🎙️ Trace the telephone game:

3 · the telephone game 
Find the original study, then show me how the headline and the social media version each changed the claim. Where did the exaggeration creep in?

How to get the most out of it

🌱 If you are newer paste the claim EXACTLY as you saw it, in quotes. The precise wording ("more than your boss", "5,000 people") is what Claude checks against the real study, so do not paraphrase it.
⚡ If you are a power user save this 4-step chain as a Claude Project or a reusable prompt, then feed it any screenshot of a "study says" post. You can also ask Claude for the study's DOI and open the primary source directly, so you are never trusting a summary of a summary.
the honest bit

When this is overkill

You do not need this for obvious opinions or clearly labelled jokes. It shines on confident, specific, numbers-heavy claims ("a study of X people found Y"), which are exactly the ones that go viral and are most likely to be bent. And remember: Claude points you to the source, you still do the final read yourself before you repeat it publicly.
