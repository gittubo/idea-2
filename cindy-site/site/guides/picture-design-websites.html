5 design websites that’ll take your designs to the next level

Hey, it's Cindy 🌱 Design websites to learn from, plus a prompt that turns three references into a headline, layout and brief.

You can have a folder full of beautiful references and still feel completely stuck when you open a blank page. The useful part is knowing which decision each reference helps you make.

These five websites cover visual direction, typography, colour, texture and advertising. Here’s how to use them together to turn a vague idea into a brief you can actually build from, whether you’re making a website, a carousel or a launch graphic.

part 01

Start with one small design

Choose something real: a landing-page hero for your next workshop, for example. Write down who it is for, what they should understand and what they should do next. Keep your first version to a headline, a short explanation, one button and one visual.

For the worked example below, we’re designing a fictional workshop called “Build your first useful AI workflow.” Its audience is busy small-business owners. Its action is joining a waitlist. We want the design to feel warm, clear and practical. Those decisions give the references a job.

You need a browser and a design tool you already use. Figma, Canva or a simple webpage will work. Public galleries can be browsed without buying a course; some services require accounts for saving, and some fonts or features cost money. None of these sites gives you blanket ownership of every image you see.

part 02

1. Savee: make your direction specific

Open Savee. It lets you collect and organise visual inspiration. Create an account if you want to save references, and check its current plan limits before relying on a large shared library.

For this exercise, select three references: one for composition, one for photography and one for the overall mood. Give each a sentence explaining what you want to borrow. “Large headline with plenty of breathing room” is useful. “Looks cool” doesn’t tell your designer what to do.

In our workshop example, you could choose a roomy editorial layout, a softly lit desk photo and a muted colour treatment. The result is a direction that can survive changes to the actual pictures.

Keep the original source links with your selections. Use the references to communicate decisions, then create or license the finished assets separately. Saving something to a board does not turn it into stock photography.

part 03

2. Typewolf: choose type by looking at real words

Open Typewolf. Its featured websites identify the typefaces being used, which makes it easier to understand why a heading and body font work together. Some learning products are paid, and featured fonts have their own prices and licences.

Pick one example where the hierarchy feels right. Notice the differences between headline, paragraph and button: size, weight, spacing and line length. Then test your actual headline in your design tool. A pair that looks beautiful in a short brand name may feel awkward in a twelve-word title.

For the workshop, try an expressive heading with a quieter body face. Start with two weights rather than adding more fonts whenever something feels flat. If your budget is limited, use Typewolf’s free-font resources to find alternatives and confirm availability with the original font provider.

Keep a screenshot of your real headline beside the reference. You should be choosing for your message, not just the specimen.

part 04

3. Happy Hues: give every colour a job

Open Happy Hues. The useful feature is seeing colour palettes applied to page elements, with the associated hex values. You can inspect the background, text and accent as a working combination.

Choose a palette and assign roles in your own file: page background, main text, secondary text and action colour. Avoid pulling five attractive swatches into a page without deciding how they work together.

For a warm workshop design, you might use cream as the main surface, charcoal for reading and a warm orange for emphasis. This is our proposed direction, not a claim that those are the values in a specific Happy Hues example.

Test the colours with your actual type size. A pale accent may look lovely as a large block but be difficult to read as a tiny label. Check the final text/background combinations with a contrast checker before publishing, especially buttons and small explanatory text.

part 05

4. fffuel: make one detail that fits

Open nnnoise for an SVG noise texture, or browse fffuel for generators such as sssurf. The controls let you make an asset for your composition instead of trying to force a downloaded graphic into it.

Start with a finished layout, then add a single restrained texture or shape. In the workshop example, put a subtle grain behind the illustration or around the edge of the hero. Keep the body copy on a quiet surface.

Adjust the generator, export the result and test it at the size people will actually see. If the graphic draws attention before the headline, reduce its opacity or remove it. Check the linked licence for the chosen output and retain the source in your project notes.

part 06

5. Adfolio: study how the message is organised

Open Adfolio. Its B2B ad collection is useful for seeing headlines, product demonstrations and calls to action together. A gallery appearance is not evidence that an ad converted well.

Choose an example with a similar communication job. If your workshop helps people finish a task, study an ad that makes the result visible. Break it into three parts: what grabs attention, what proves the offer and what asks for the next action.

For our workshop, that might become a specific headline, a simple preview of the workflow someone will build, and a “Join the waitlist” button. Write your own copy and use your own product visual. You are studying the structure, not reproducing another company’s campaign.

part 07

Turn your references into a buildable brief

Paste this into Claude with the references or screenshots you have permission to share. If it cannot open a link, supply the screenshot and your notes instead.

copy this prompt
Act as my design director. Turn these references into a concrete brief for one landing-page hero.

Offer: Build your first useful AI workflow, a fictional workshop.
Audience: busy small-business owners.
Goal: explain the workshop and invite people to join the waitlist.
Feeling: warm, clear and practical.
Assets available: [list your own or licensed assets].
References and what I like: [paste links, screenshots and notes].

Return:
1. A headline, supporting sentence and button label.
2. A layout described from top to bottom, including mobile order.
3. Heading/body type roles, colour roles and spacing guidance.
4. One optional decorative asset and exactly where it belongs.
5. A source list separating inspiration from reusable assets.
6. Three checks I can perform on the first draft.

Do not invent testimonials, performance results or personal experience. Do not claim a reference asset is licensed for reuse unless its actual licence supports that. Make a specific recommendation when my notes conflict, and explain the tradeoff briefly.

Your first action is to choose the small design and save three references with clear notes. Stop browsing once you can explain the intended result.

On the first draft, check whether the headline is the first thing you notice, whether the page makes sense on your phone and whether the button describes a clear next step. If it still feels messy, remove one visual and simplify the type hierarchy before adding anything else. The finished payoff is a usable brief and a clearer design, not another hundred bookmarks.
