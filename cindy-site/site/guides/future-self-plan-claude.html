the idea

What you are building

That 10-year visualization hits hard for a day, then the feeling fades and nothing changes. The fix is to make the regret do paperwork: you hand Claude the scary picture once, and it becomes a plan you run on repeat.

1. The regret your honest worst-case life in 10 years, described in real detail.
2. The 5 decisions Claude reverse-engineers the small habits happening now that quietly lead there.
3. This-week habits each decision flipped into one tiny thing you can start this week.
4. The weekly system those habits stacked, tracked, and reviewed every Sunday, so it runs itself.
paste this first

The future-self prompt

Open Claude, paste this, and fill in the bracket honestly. The more specific your worst-case, the sharper the plan comes back.

⏳ the future-self prompt 
I'm doing a future-self exercise with you, and I need you to be specific and honest, not generic motivation. Here is the worst-case version of my life 10 years from now, if I keep drifting exactly the way I am right now: [describe it in vivid detail: the work I'm stuck in, my relationships, my health and energy, my money, where I live, and above all how a normal Tuesday actually feels]. Treat this like a pre-mortem: assume that future has already happened, and work backwards from it. First, reverse-engineer the 5 small, believable decisions or daily habits I'm making RIGHT NOW that quietly compound into that exact outcome, and be specific to what I described instead of listing generic vices. Then flip each of those 5 into one tiny habit I could realistically start THIS week without overhauling my life. Turn those into a one-page weekly checklist, and for each habit add a short Sunday check-in question I can answer honestly. Keep it realistic and kind, never drill-sergeant, and ask me one clarifying question before you start if anything about my situation is unclear.
This prompt is doing two things researchers already trust. Describing the future as if it has already happened is a pre-mortem (Gary Klein, HBR): imagining an outcome as done, not just possible, makes people about 30% better at naming the real reasons for it. Working backwards from that future to today is backcasting, which is exactly why "reverse-engineer the 5 decisions" surfaces things a normal goals list never would.
how it runs

The 4 steps

  1. 🌱 Describe the worst-case. Write the 10-year future you do not want in brutal, specific detail. This is the raw material, so do not rush it or keep it vague. "Vaguely unhappy" gives you a vague plan back.
  2. 🔍 Let Claude reverse-engineer it. It traces that future back to five small decisions you are quietly making now. Seeing the actual dominoes, in your own words, is the part that makes it click.
  3. 🌱 Flip each into a this-week habit. Not "get fit", but "10-minute walk after lunch on work days". Tiny and specific beats ambitious and abandoned, every single time.
  4. 🗓️ Turn it into a weekly system. Claude formats the habits into a one-page checklist with a Sunday check-in, so the plan has a home instead of living in your head where it evaporates.
what it looks like filled in

A worked example

So it feels real, here is what one honest run can look like end to end.

Worst case (10 years out): "I'm still in the same job I keep meaning to leave, coasting and quietly bored. I'm out of shape and tired by 2pm most days. I have no savings buffer, so I can't take any risks. My close friendships have thinned out because I always say I'm too busy, and a normal Tuesday feels like autopilot."

The 5 decisions quietly leading there: scrolling instead of applying for anything new, skipping movement because the day gets away from me, spending to the edge of every paycheck, leaving messages on read for weeks, and saying yes to busywork while saying no to the things that actually matter to me.

Three this-week habits it flipped them into: apply to one interesting role every Sunday night, take a 10-minute walk right after lunch on work days, and move $50 to savings the morning after payday. Tiny, dated, and hard to argue with.

shape it to your life

Make it actually fit you

Run these in the same chat, in order, to bend the plan around your real week instead of your ideal one.

🔧 shrink it to reality 
Be honest with me: that checklist is more than I can actually sustain in a busy, tired week. Cut it down to the 2 or 3 habits with the highest leverage on the future I described, and tell me plainly why you kept those and dropped the rest. For each survivor, shrink it until it feels almost too easy to skip, the two-minute version I'd still do on my worst day (so "read more" becomes "read one page in bed"). I would rather do three tiny things every week for a year than ten ambitious things for nine days, so bias hard toward what will still be running in three months.
🔗 anchor each habit to a routine 
Now anchor each of these habits to something I already do automatically every day, so I don't have to rely on memory or motivation. First ask me for my current daily routine (when I wake, my coffee, my commute, when I sit down to work, dinner, bed), then rewrite each habit in the format "After [existing habit I already do], I will [new tiny habit]." Pick anchors that naturally sit right before the moment the new habit makes sense, and flag any anchor that's too vague or unreliable to actually trigger the new behavior. Give me the final stacked list I can read in ten seconds.
🎯 turn each into an if-then plan 
Convert each habit into an if-then plan that names the exact situation, so my brain already knows what to do when the moment arrives. Use the format "If [specific time, place, or trigger], then I will [specific action]," and make the trigger concrete enough that there's no decision left to make in the moment (not "if I have time" but "if it's 1pm and I've finished lunch"). Then, for each one, add a backup if-then for the most likely thing that will get in the way, in the format "If [obstacle happens], then I will [smaller fallback action]." Give me the plans and their backups as one clean list.
These two prompts aren't random. Anchoring a new habit to one you never skip is habit stacking (James Clear's "after I do X, I will do Y"), which borrows the automatic momentum you already have. The if-then format is an implementation intention: across 94 studies, deciding the exact when-and-where in advance had a medium-to-large effect on follow-through, because it removes the in-the-moment decision that willpower usually loses.
run it weekly

Make it run on autopilot

This is the part that turns a nice reflection into a system. Set the tracker up once, then it is a five-minute Sunday ritual from there.

🗂️ build the Notion tracker 
Turn this final habit list into a simple weekly habit-tracker table I can paste straight into Notion. Give me one row per habit and one column for each day of the week (Mon to Sun) so I can check them off, plus a "target" column for how many days a week I'm aiming for and a "notes" column for what got in the way. Keep the habit names short enough to read at a glance, put the highest-leverage habit in the top row, and format it as a clean markdown table with no extra commentary so it pastes in cleanly.
📅 the Sunday check-in 
It's Sunday and I'm doing my weekly check-in. Here's how the week actually went, habit by habit: [paste your wins, misses, and what got in the way]. Hold me accountable kindly, with no shame and no lecture. Tell me which habits are clearly working and worth protecting, which one I should shrink because I keep missing it, and whether anything should be dropped entirely because it isn't moving me toward the future I described. Then rewrite next week's checklist with those adjustments baked in, and end with one small, specific thing to look forward to about doing it next week.
🔭 the monthly zoom-out 
It's been about a month of running this system. Zoom out with me: based on these four weeks of check-ins [paste or summarize how the month went], am I actually drifting away from the worst-case future I described, or just going through the motions? Point to the specific evidence in what I've told you, name the one habit that's created the most real change, and name the one I've been quietly avoiding and why that might be. Then propose one new tiny habit to add for next month and one to retire because it's done its job or isn't earning its place. Be honest even if it's uncomfortable.

And for the week you inevitably fall off, because everyone does:

🫶 restart without shame 
I fell off this system and I've done nothing for [X days or weeks]. I'm tempted to feel like I've blown it and quit entirely, which is exactly how this usually dies. Talk me down from the all-or-nothing spiral, remind me in one line why I started, and do not make me restart the whole checklist. Instead, pick the single easiest habit from my list, shrink it to the smallest possible version, and give me one thing I can do in the next hour to get back on the board today. Then rebuild the rest gradually across this week rather than all at once.
why it works

The part most people miss

The feeling fades, the system does not. The reason the January reset never sticks is that you keep the bright vision and skip the friction, so your brain quietly files the goal as already done. Holding the vision against the real obstacle is WOOP (wish, outcome, obstacle, plan), Gabriele Oettingen's 20-year line of research, and it beats pure positive thinking in study after study. You only have to feel the regret once. After that it's a checklist and a five-minute Sunday review, and Claude does the weekly thinking for you.
a soft note

Keep it kind

This is a planning and motivation exercise, not therapy. If the 3am spirals are more than a one-off, please talk to someone you trust or a real professional. The prompt is here to help you build, not to sit in the dark part.
