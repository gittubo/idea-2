step 1

Find a business that needs one

This is the part that turns it into income, not just a cool build. Open Google Maps and look for a local business with amazing reviews but no website (or a really dated one). Those owners already have demand, they're just invisible online, so a premium site is an easy yes.

  1. 🗺️ Search Google Maps for a category in your area (cafe, barber, nail salon, dentist, gym, trades). Look for great reviews and a missing or broken "Website" link, that gap is the opportunity.
  2. 📸 Grab one product photo. Honestly, any decent image of what they sell works, you will use it to steer the visuals. Screenshot it from Maps, their Instagram, or a review.
  3. 📋 Copy their public info. Name, what they sell, location, hours, price range, and a line or two from their best reviews. Drop it all into a note, this is what ChatGPT builds from.
🎯 Pick one you would actually be proud to send. A business with real reviews and no site is the perfect first client, they can see exactly what they are missing the second you send the link.

step 3

Steal the look: break down a reference visual

Before you generate anything, teach ChatGPT what premium looks like for this business. Find one visual you love (an ad, a hero animation, the burger transformation from the reel), screenshot it, paste it into the chat, and have ChatGPT reverse-engineer it:

🔬 the visual breakdown prompt 
Analyze this reference visual like a creative director. Break it down into a generation recipe: the subject and framing, the lighting and mood, the colour palette with hex codes, the camera behaviour, and the one transformation that makes it feel premium. Then rewrite that recipe as a ready-to-run Higgsfield video prompt for MY business's product: keep the same energy but make it original to this brand, one continuous shot with no cuts, locked-off camera, clean background, highest resolution available.
This step is why your output won't look generic. You're not asking for "a nice burger video", you're handing the plugin a shot-by-shot recipe with your client's actual product in it.
step 4

Paste the website plan prompt

This is the engine of the whole thing. Paste in the business info you gathered in step 1, then this prompt. ChatGPT plans the full site from it: brand direction, structure, real copy, and a shot list for every visual. Let it ask you for anything it's missing.

📋 the website plan prompt 
You are a senior brand and web designer AND a no-code implementation expert. I want to build a premium one-page website for a local business that looks like it cost thousands AND actually works (forms, bookings, payments, and more, all wired up with real tools). Interview me first, then produce a complete build plan.

THE BUSINESS: [describe it in a sentence or two: name, what they sell, city, and the vibe]. Before you plan anything, ask me for whatever you are missing, one batch at a time: the offering, location, opening hours, rough price range, the feeling they want customers to have, any existing brand colours or logo, and how they currently take enquiries or bookings (phone, DMs, walk-ins, a booking app). Wait for my answers, then continue.

Then produce, in this order:

1. BRAND DIRECTION: a wordmark treatment, a 4 to 5 colour palette with hex codes (appetising and premium, never generic), two font pairings (one display, one body, using fonts that are free on Google Fonts), and a one-line brand voice.

2. SITE MAP: the sections of a single premium landing page, in order, and the one job of each: hero, signature items or services, the story or craft, social proof, location and hours, and a clear call to action. Only what a real small business needs, nothing padded.

3. THE HERO MOMENT: describe one premium, scroll-driven animated hero that makes the site feel expensive and is completely true to this business. It should be a single continuous shot the page scrubs through as the visitor scrolls (for a food business, the product assembling itself so you can see what is inside; adapt the idea for a salon, gym, or trades business). Specify exactly what it shows, how it moves, and why it fits.

4. COPY: write the real, on-brand copy for every section: headlines, subheads, item or service descriptions, the story, social proof framing, and the call to action. No lorem ipsum, no filler, match the brand voice exactly.

5. THE HIGGSFIELD VISUAL ASSET BRIEF: list every image and animation the site needs, each written as a ready-to-run generation prompt for the Higgsfield plugin: the scroll hero animation, 3 to 6 product or service shots, one atmosphere or interior shot, and any brand texture. For each, specify subject, framing, lighting, mood, and aspect ratio, so the whole set looks like one cohesive shoot.

6. FUNCTIONALITY PLAN (do this properly): First tell me in one or two plain sentences that a single static HTML page cannot, on its own, store data, send email, take a booking, or process a payment. Every dynamic feature is added by connecting a named third-party tool that handles that part, while the page stays one simple file the owner can host anywhere. Then ask me which of these the business actually needs: contact form, bookings, online ordering, payments, a map, analytics, newsletter signup. For ONLY the ones I pick, give me a short block each covering: what it does for this business, the exact named tool to use, in one line how it attaches to a plain static HTML page, the real 2026 cost tier, and the honest limit. Recommend the best fit for this specific business; use the options below unless a better one clearly fits, and note that prices change so I should confirm on the tool's own pricing page.
   - Contact form: Formspree (free for 50 submissions per month, paid from about US$15/mo). Point the form's action at a Formspree endpoint, no server needed. If the site is hosted on Netlify, Netlify Forms works too (free for 100 submissions per month, just add data-netlify to the form). Limit: submissions run through the form service and free tiers cap the monthly count.
   - Bookings and appointments: Calendly (free for 1 event type, Standard about US$10 per seat per month), embedded as an inline widget or popup link. For a salon or clinic taking deposits and juggling staff, use Square Appointments (free for a single user). Limit: the free Calendly plan is one event type with Calendly branding, and the calendar lives on their platform.
   - Online ordering: Square Online (free plan, no monthly fee, about 3.3% plus 30c per online order as of January 2026), or link out to an existing UberEats or DoorDash page. Add an "Order now" button that opens the hosted store. Limit: the page hands the order off to Square, which takes a fee per order.
   - Payments (deposits, gift cards, pay in advance): Stripe Payment Links or the Stripe Buy Button (no monthly fee, about 1.75% plus A$0.30 per domestic card in Australia, about 2.9% plus 30c in the US). Paste the payment link into a button or embed the Buy Button. Limit: checkout is hosted securely by Stripe so the customer briefly leaves the page, and Stripe takes a fee per transaction.
   - Map: Google Maps embed (free). In Google Maps choose Share, then "Embed a map", copy the iframe, and paste it in. Limit: the free embed is a standard pinned map; a custom-styled map needs the paid Google Maps Platform.
   - Analytics: Google Analytics 4 (free), pasted as a tag in the head. Privacy-friendly alternative: Cloudflare Web Analytics (free, no cookie banner). Limit: GA4 uses cookies so add a short privacy note, and data only appears once visitors arrive.
   - Newsletter signup: Kit, formerly ConvertKit (free for up to 10,000 subscribers), embedded as a signup form. Alternative: Mailchimp (free for 250 contacts and 500 sends a month). Limit: emails send from the provider, not the page, and free tiers add branding.
   Close this section with one honest line: none of these need coding, but each is a real account the owner (or I) must set up, and the free tiers are enough to launch and prove the site before anyone pays for an upgrade.

7. BUILD NOTES: mobile-first layout notes, where each visual and each functionality embed goes, a page title and meta description for SEO, the accessibility basics (colour contrast, alt text, tap-target size), performance notes to keep it fast, and a note to keep the scroll hero optimised for web (compressed, with a muted autoplay loop as a fallback if scroll-scrubbing is not possible, and a lighter version for mobile).

8. HANDOVER PACKAGE: write the final site as a single, clean, heavily commented HTML file that a non-technical owner can maintain later with ChatGPT or any AI assistant. Group every piece of editable text (business name, hours, prices, phone, menu or service items) near the top under one clearly labelled block, and clearly label where each functionality embed lives (the form, the booking widget, the payment button, the map, the analytics tag, the newsletter form) so the owner knows exactly what to swap if they change tools. At the very top of the file, add a short plain-English comment titled "HOW TO EDIT YOUR SITE" that explains, in normal words, how to change the text, the hours, and the photos, and how to update each connected tool.

Output the whole thing as one clean brief I can build from, keeping every dynamic feature honest about cost and limits, then offer to generate the site's full HTML and CSS from it next.
💡 Get specific in the bracket. "A craft burger shop in Fitzroy, wood-fired, a bit premium, for people who care about where their food comes from" gives you a far better plan than "a burger shop." The more real detail you feed it, the less generic everything downstream looks.
step 5

Generate the premium visuals with Higgsfield

Step 4's plan hands you a shot list written as prompts. Now run them, one at a time, right there in ChatGPT, and you can watch the visuals get generated live as it goes. The hero animation is the piece that sells the whole site, so start there.

🎬 the hero animation (via Higgsfield) 
Using Higgsfield, generate the hero animation as one continuous shot with no cuts: the [product, e.g. signature stacked burger] starts as separated floating layers, then assembles itself piece by piece and lands inside the brand's packaging [or: onto the plate], on a clean dark background. Locked-off camera, cinematic appetising lighting, smooth continuous motion, highest resolution available, wide and vertical versions. The single unbroken take matters: the website will scrub through this shot as visitors scroll, so every in-between frame needs to exist.
📸 the matching photo set (via Higgsfield) 
Using Higgsfield, generate 4 product shots that match the hero: [list the items from your plan]. Same lighting and mood as the hero, dark premium background, consistent style so they look like one shoot. Square and landscape crops. Then one wide atmosphere shot of the space (warm, inviting, editorial).
🎯 Consistency is the premium tell. Ask for the same lighting and background across every asset, and generate a few variations of each, then keep the ones that match. A cohesive set is what separates an expensive-looking site from a stock-photo one.
step 6

Build it, ship it free, and send it

You have the plan and the visuals. Now turn it into a real page. Back in the same chat, paste this:

🏗️ build the page 
Now build this as a single responsive HTML file with clean CSS, using the copy and the sections from the plan. Leave clearly labelled placeholders where each Higgsfield asset goes. Make the hero scroll-driven: pin the hero video full-screen and map scroll progress to the video's playback time, with smoothing, so the transformation plays as the visitor scrolls, and fall back to a muted autoplay loop if that isn't possible. Keep the headline to one line placed above the product so it never covers it, pull the colour palette from the brand's packaging, and keep everything optimised so the page loads fast. Comment the code in plain English, group all the editable text (name, hours, prices, contact) at the top, and add a short HOW TO EDIT note as the very first comment, so the owner can update it later with any AI assistant.
  1. 🖼️ Drop in your visuals. Save the Higgsfield hero animation and photos and place them into the labelled spots. If wiring up the hero video feels fiddly, just ask ChatGPT to do it for you in plain English.
  2. 🌐 Put it online free. Publish the whole project folder, the HTML file plus its media folder kept together (drag only the HTML and the images break), onto Netlify Drop or Cloudflare Pages. You now have a live link to send the client. For a paid build, connect the client's own domain (theirshop.com) in the host's settings, about $10 to $20 a year for the name.
  3. ✏️ Tweak in plain English. Anything you want changed, describe it back to ChatGPT: "make the hero taller on mobile", "warm up the palette", "add an order button to the header". No code needed.
  4. 📩 Send it to the business. Message the owner with the live link: "I built your shop a website, no strings, here's the link." A finished premium site they can see beats any pitch, and it's how you turn a build into a paying client.
📱 Test the hero on a real phone before you send it. Scroll-scrubbing the video is smooth on desktop, but most phones will just play the autoplay loop instead, which still looks great. To make desktop scrubbing buttery, tell ChatGPT to re-encode the hero video with frequent keyframes (or use an image sequence). Always open the live link on your own phone first.
the honest part

What a static site can't do on its own

The page ChatGPT and Higgsfield build is the storefront: it looks expensive and loads fast. But on its own, a static page cannot take a booking, process a payment, or email you an enquiry. That is true of every website, the smart parts are always a small tool you plug in. Add only the ones this business actually needs, and each is a free or cheap snippet you can ask ChatGPT to drop into the page.

📩 Contact form A static page can't email you a submission by itself. Wire in Formspree (free for 50 a month) or Netlify Forms (free) and enquiries land in the owner's inbox.
📅 Bookings Let customers self-book with Calendly (free for one event type) or Square Appointments (free for a solo operator). Embed the widget or link a "Book now" button.
💳 Payments and ordering The page can never handle a card itself. Point a button at a Stripe payment link or a free Square Online store; they take the money and a small fee per sale.
🗺️ Map and reviews Paste a free Google Maps embed for the location, and drop in two or three real review quotes with a button to their Google listing for instant social proof.
📊 Analytics Add one free tag (Google Analytics 4 or Cloudflare Web Analytics) to the page so the owner can see how many people visit and where they came from.
🌐 Domain and hosting Hosting stays free on Netlify or Cloudflare Pages. The one real cost is the domain name, about $10 to $20 a year, so the site lives at theirshop.com.
🚫 Be honest when you quote. The build is one price, the running tools are the client's (a domain, plus any paid tier if they grow). And the moment a form collects a name or email, that is personal data: only collect what the business needs, and add a one-line privacy note near the form. Free tiers and prices change, so check each tool's own pricing page before you promise a number.
turn it into income

How to actually get paid for this

1. Build one on spec Pick a local business you like that has a bad site (or none), build them a premium version, and send the live link cold. A finished site they can see beats any pitch.
2. Price the outcome A one-page premium site with custom visuals is commonly $800 to $2,500 for a simple build, up to $5,000 or more with a booking or menu system. Quote the result, not your hours.
3. Offer a care plan Small monthly retainer for updates, new seasonal photos, and hosting. That's the recurring part most freelancers forget to sell.
4. Let the work sell itself Post the before-and-after. A premium site you built in an afternoon is the whole portfolio, and the whole ad.
the handover

Hand it over so they can own it

Sell them a site they can actually run, not one they are locked out of. Because you built the whole thing in ChatGPT, the owner can keep editing it the same way, no developer needed.

  1. 📂 Give them the file. Hand over the single HTML file and the visuals, plus the plain-English "how to edit" note ChatGPT wrote at the top of it. That file is theirs to keep.
  2. 💬 Show them the plain-English edit. New hours, a price change, a new photo: they paste the file into ChatGPT and describe the change in normal words, exactly how you built it. No code touched.
  3. 🔁 Or keep it on a care plan. If they would rather not touch it at all, that is your recurring income: a small monthly fee to make the updates and host it for them.
💡 Set expectations up front. The owner gets the most out of this if they are a little comfortable with AI tools, since editing the site means talking to an AI assistant. Say it on day one: it keeps you from becoming their unpaid help desk, and it makes the site genuinely theirs.
the honest bit

Keep it real

🚫 Deliver something you'd be proud to sign. AI gets you 90% of the way fast, but you still own the taste: check the copy is true to the business, the visuals are consistent, and every link works before you send a client anything.
💡 The hero animation is the whole trick. If you only nail one thing, make it that. A single premium moment on an otherwise clean page reads as "expensive" far more than a busy site with ten effects.
