why it works

The demo is the entire sale

A normal cold email asks a stranger for trust before you have earned any. You say you build good websites, they have heard that from nine other people this month, and nothing happens. A demo flips the order. You show them a working version of their own business, looking better, with their real name and services on it. There is nothing to imagine and nothing to take on faith, which is why the reply rate is not remotely comparable.

The reason this used to stay theoretical is that building a bespoke demo for a stranger took a day, so nobody did it for a lead who might not answer. That is the actual change. Research, audit, build and draft now happen in one run, so the effort per lead drops far enough that showing proof first becomes the normal way to reach out.

The exact multi-step prompt

One prompt, five stages. The instruction to stop and show you the shortlist matters: it is the checkpoint that stops Claude spending twenty minutes building a demo for a business you would never want to work with.

🎯 the cold lead to client prompt 
I sell [what you actually sell, for example "website redesigns for local service businesses, $2,500 to $6,000"] and I want you to find me one real prospect and build the whole pitch. Work through these five stages in order, in this one session, and save every output into this folder as you go.

STAGE 1, research. Find businesses of this type in this area: [business type] in [suburb or city]. Look at somewhere between fifteen and twenty five of them using their websites, their Google listings and their social profiles. For each one, note the business name, the website address, the review count and rating, roughly when the site looks like it was last touched, and whether it appears to be run by an owner rather than a chain. Save this as a table.

STAGE 2, shortlist. From that table, pick the three best targets using this filter: strong reviews (a real signal that the business itself works and has money coming in) but a website that is clearly holding them back. Rank them, and for each one write two sentences on why they are worth my time and one sentence on the risk that they say no. Stop here and show me the three before you go further, and tell me which one you would pick and why.

STAGE 3, audit. Once I confirm the target, audit their website properly. Go through: what the site does and does not tell a first-time visitor in the first five seconds, whether there is any social proof on the page at all, where the call to action sits and whether it is findable on a phone, how the site behaves on mobile, page speed and anything obviously heavy, whether basic contact and booking details are one tap away, and whether the copy talks about the customer or only about the business. Write it as a short report with the specific issue, why it costs them customers in plain non-technical language, and the fix. No jargon, no scores out of ten, no padding. If something is genuinely fine, say it is fine, because a fake problem is worse than no email.

STAGE 4, demo. Build a single-page modern website for this exact business using their real name, services, location, hours and contact details taken from their existing site and listing. It should be responsive, fast, accessible, and it must fix the specific issues from the audit rather than just looking prettier. Use plain HTML and CSS in one folder so I can host it anywhere. Do not invent testimonials, prices, awards or claims: if their real site does not say it, it does not go on the demo. Use a neutral placeholder for their logo rather than copying brand assets.

STAGE 5, pitch email. Draft a short outreach email from me to this business. Under 150 words, plain language, no flattery opener, no "I hope this email finds you well". Lead with the single most costly issue from the audit and what it is doing to them, mention that I already built a working version of the fix, and put the demo link in as the entire call to action. Sign it with my real name and business details: [your name, business name, ABN or registration number, phone, and the address details you are legally required to include]. Add a plain line telling them they can reply to opt out of any further contact from me.

Finally, give me a checklist of every factual claim you made across the audit and the email, with where each one came from, so I can verify all of them before I send anything.
pick well

What actually makes a good target

The filter is doing more work than the prompt is. Great reviews plus a weak website is not a random combination, it is specifically the business that has proven demand and a leak in the funnel.

⭐ Strong reviews, thin website Reviews prove people pay them and like them. A bad site means the gap is presentation, not product, which is a problem you can actually fix.
👤 Owner operated One decision maker, no procurement, no brand guidelines from head office. A chain location cannot say yes to you even if they want to.
💰 High value per customer Dentists, physios, trades, law, cosmetic clinics. If one new customer is worth thousands, a website fix pays for itself in weeks and the pitch writes itself.
📵 Visibly broken on a phone Text you have to pinch to read, a phone number that does not dial, a booking button below three screens of scroll. Easy for them to verify in ten seconds on their own phone.
💡 Stay in one niche for a whole batch. Your second physio pitch reuses the audit criteria, the demo layout and most of the email from your first one, so lead five takes a fraction of the time lead one did.
the six checks

What the audit is actually looking for

🕐 The five second test Can a stranger tell what this business does, where it is, and what to do next, without scrolling? Most local sites fail on the "where" and the "next".
🗣️ Social proof on the page Hundreds of Google reviews and not one of them shown on the homepage is the most common miss, and the easiest win in the whole demo.
👉 Call to action placement Book, call or enquire should be visible without scrolling, and repeated at the bottom. Buried CTAs are the leak the owner can feel but cannot name.
📱 Mobile behaviour Most local searches happen on a phone. Tap targets too small, text too small, a menu that does not open. Check it on your own phone, not just a narrow browser window.
⚡ Speed and weight Huge unoptimised hero images and a stack of plugins. Report it as "your page takes seven seconds on 4G and people leave", not as a technical metric.
✍️ Who the copy is about "Established in 1998 with a commitment to excellence" tells a customer nothing. Rewriting it around what the customer gets is free and it demos beautifully.
make it real

Get the demo online so the link works

An attached zip file will not get opened, so the demo needs a live URL before you send anything. Two genuinely free routes:

  1. 🪄 Drag and drop it. Take the folder Claude built and drop it onto Netlify Drop. You get a live URL in about thirty seconds with no setup, and the free tier covers a demo comfortably.
  2. 📦 Or use GitHub Pages. Push the folder to a public repo and turn on GitHub Pages. Slower to set up the first time, but free and it stays put.
  3. 🏷️ Label it as a concept. Put a small line in the footer saying it is an unofficial concept by you, with your name on it. It is honest, it protects you, and it makes the ownership obvious if the page gets shared around their team.
  4. 📸 Screenshot it too. A before and after image in the email body means they see the point even if they never click. Two screenshots side by side does most of the persuading on its own.
⚠️ Do not copy their logo, their photography or their exact branding onto the demo. Use a neutral placeholder and their business name in text. Recreating their brand assets to pitch them is a bad first impression and an easy thing to avoid.
the send

Tighten the email before it goes

Claude's first draft is usually one notch too keen. Run it through this second prompt before you send, and read the result out loud.

✉️ the email tightening pass 
Here is the pitch email you drafted: [paste it]. Cut it down and make it sound like a person, not a template.

Rules: get it under 120 words. Delete every sentence that is about me rather than about them. Open on the specific thing I noticed on their site and what it is costing them, in their language, not marketing language. One link only, to the demo, and make clicking it the only thing I am asking for. No "I hope this finds you well", no "just following up", no exclamation marks, no em dashes, and do not use the words leverage, elevate, seamless or partner.

Keep my legal sender details and the opt-out line intact, they are not optional.

Then give me three subject lines under seven words each: one that names their business, one that names the specific problem, and one that is plainly curious without being clickbait. Tell me which you would send and why.
💡 Send it as a real one to one email from your normal address, not through a bulk sending tool. It arrives better, it is easier to reply to, and at this volume you are having a conversation rather than running a campaign.
the close

When they reply, this is the bit that turns it into money

A demo gets you a conversation, not a contract. Most people who get this far then wing the call, quote a number they regret, and end up building a whole site for the price of the demo. Fifteen minutes of prep fixes that.

  1. 📞 Ask for a fifteen minute call, not a proposal. A proposal written before you understand their business is a guess. On a short call you find out what they actually want the site to do, which is what you are really pricing.
  2. ❓ Lead with the business question. "How do most of your new customers find you right now, and what happens when they land on the site?" Their answer tells you whether this is a website job, a booking-flow job or a copy job.
  3. 💷 Price the outcome, not the pages. A local business that gets one new customer a week from a fixed site is not comparing you to a template marketplace. Give one number, one inclusions list, and a clear start date.
  4. ✍️ Send a one page scope the same day. What is included, what is not, timeline, payment split (a deposit up front is normal and protects both of you), and what you need from them to start.
💼 prep the call and price it 
I have a call booked with [business name] after they replied to the demo site I built for them. Here is the audit I did and the demo I sent: [paste the audit, describe the demo]. Get me ready.

Write me a call plan: the five questions that will tell me what this project actually needs, in the order I should ask them, with a one line note on what each answer would change about my quote. Then list the three objections most likely to come up for a business of this size (price, timing, "my nephew built the current one"), with a straight answer to each that is not defensive and does not discount on the spot.

Then draft the follow-up: a one page scope of work with what is included, what is explicitly not included, a realistic timeline in weeks, and a payment split with a deposit. Leave the price as a blank for me to fill in, but tell me the range you think fits this business and the reasoning, based on what a new customer is plausibly worth to them. Flag anything in my audit that I should soften on the call because it might sound like criticism of work they paid for.
know the rules

The legal bit, briefly

Unsolicited commercial email is regulated, and the rules vary by country. In Australia the Spam Act requires consent before you send a commercial message, and inferred consent is narrower than most people assume. Every commercial message must also accurately identify you and your business, carry correct contact details, and include a clear unsubscribe option that you honour within five working days. The ACMA is explicit that the burden of proving consent sits with the sender: avoid sending spam. Read the rules for wherever your prospect is before you start a batch, keep the volume low and genuinely personal, and always give them a one line way to tell you to stop.

The version of this that works long term is not a thousand automated emails. It is five carefully chosen businesses a week, each with a real demo built for them, each one you would happily have a phone call about. That is also, conveniently, the version that stays on the right side of every rule above.
