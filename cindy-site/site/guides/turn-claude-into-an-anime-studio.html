the setup

What you're actually building

Two roles, one chat.

Claude is the director

Writes the story, designs the cast, breaks your scene into shots, keeps every character consistent, and sharpens each prompt before anything generates.

Higgsfield is the studio

Draws the character art, animates the shots, and does the voices. It's the part that actually renders pixels.

For context on what this stands in for: Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry costs a single TV anime episode at roughly ¥25 million, about $150,000 USD, with a 12-episode season landing around $2 to $3 million (METI production cascade, 2025 and CREATIVE VILLAGE, Nov 2025). You're not going to match a real studio. What you can do is get a watchable animated scene out of an afternoon, which wasn't possible on your own two years ago.

one minute, once

Connect Higgsfield to Claude

You add Higgsfield as a custom connector once, then never touch settings again:

  1. Open your connector settings In Claude, go to Customize, then Connectors.
  2. Add a custom connector Click +, then Add custom connector.
  3. Name it Call it Higgsfield MCP.
  4. Paste the URL https://mcp.higgsfield.ai/mcp
  5. Connect Click Connect and sign in to your Higgsfield account. It's OAuth, so there are no API keys to generate or paste anywhere.

Three things that catch people out:

💡 You don't need a paid Claude plan. Custom connectors work on Free too, you just get one. Pro and Max are uncapped. A lot of people assume this is Pro-only and skip it (Anthropic's setup docs).
🔁 Turn it on per chat. Adding a connector doesn't switch it on everywhere. In the chat box click +, then Connectors, and toggle Higgsfield on for that conversation.
🖥️ On desktop, fully quit and reopen (Cmd+Q, not just closing the window) if the tools don't appear.
the production line

The flow, step by step

This is the order Higgsfield actually uses, and each step feeds the next. Don't skip ahead.

  1. 📸 Drop in a photo Add a photo of yourself, or of anyone you want to turn into an anime, and ask Claude for a character sheet plus help picking a genre. The photo anchors the likeness, so use a clear, well-lit, front-facing one.
  2. 🧬 Pick your character sheet Higgsfield returns options. Choose the one you like, and that sheet becomes the reference everything later points back to. This is the consistency step, and it's worth being fussy here.
  3. 🎬 Generate the scene, then choose one Ask for the scene, look at what comes back, and pick. Don't just accept the first result.
  4. 🖼️ Get every scene as a still image FIRST Before anything moves, each scene has to exist as a picture. Black and white manga panels are one way to do it, and they're brilliant for judging composition with zero distraction, but it does not have to be manga. Full-colour scenes work exactly the same way. Pick whichever suits your story. The rule that matters is the order: image first, motion second.
  5. 🌈 Then animate the frames you approved Motion comes last, layered on top of stills you've already signed off.
Why stills first is the whole trick. It separates the decisions. Composition, character consistency and staging all get settled in still frames, where changing your mind costs almost nothing. Motion is the expensive, slow part, so you only spend it on frames you've already approved. Manga panels make this even cheaper because black and white strips out colour as a variable, but a full-colour still does the same job. Skip the still stage entirely and you end up re-rendering video to fix problems you could have fixed in a drawing.

The anime studio director prompt

This is the one from the reel. Paste it into Claude with Higgsfield connected, fill the two brackets, and let it run the production properly instead of guessing shot by shot.

🎬 the director prompt 
You are my anime studio director. We are making [YOUR SCENE OR EPISODE] in the style of [SERIES OR ERA, e.g. late-2000s psychological thriller anime].

Before you generate anything, lock the production bible and show it to me:
1. LOGLINE: one sentence. What happens, and what changes by the end.
2. CAST: for every character, a locked description I can reuse word for word in every shot. Age, hair colour and style, eye colour, build, full outfit with exact colours, and one signature visual detail.
3. WORLD: location, time of day, weather, a palette of 3 to 5 colours, and where the light is coming from.
4. TONE: the emotional temperature in three words.

Then break the scene into a SHOT LIST. For each shot give me:
- Shot number and duration (15 seconds maximum, that is the model ceiling)
- Shot size: extreme wide, wide, medium, close, or extreme close
- Camera angle, and whether it moves or stays locked off
- What happens, in one sentence
- The full character description from the bible, pasted in again in full
- Lighting and palette for that specific shot
- Any dialogue, with the emotion behind the line

Rules you must follow:
- Repeat the full character description in every single shot. Never write "the same girl as before". The image model has no memory between generations.
- Keep the light source consistent across shots or the cut will feel wrong.
- Vary your shot sizes. Wide to establish, close for emotion. Never three of the same size in a row.
- Order the shots the way they will actually be cut.

Then generate shot 1, show me, and stop. Wait for my approval before moving to shot 2.

The reason this beats "make me an anime": you get a production bible first, and every later prompt quotes it, so your character stops drifting between shots.

Make any character, including the villain

Good anime casts are built on contrast. Your antagonist should read as the hero's opposite before anyone says a word.

😈 the cast builder 
Design [NUMBER] characters for my anime. The story is: [ONE LINE ABOUT YOUR STORY].

For each character give me:
- Name and role (protagonist, rival, antagonist, mentor, comic relief)
- The visual hook: the one detail someone could draw from memory
- A LOCKED APPEARANCE BLOCK I can paste into every image prompt: age, hair, eyes, build, outfit with exact colours, default posture
- A 3-colour palette, and what those colours signal about them
- Their silhouette test: if you blacked them out completely, could you still tell who it is?
- How they visually contrast the protagonist

Make the antagonist a visual inversion of the hero: opposite palette, opposite silhouette, opposite posture, opposite energy.

Then give me one ready-to-send character sheet prompt per character that I can fire straight at Higgsfield, showing them front, three-quarter and side view on a plain background.
Use it when: you're starting a new story, or your cast currently looks like the same face in different outfits.
Heads up: save the locked appearance blocks somewhere you can copy from. You'll paste them dozens of times, and changing even a few words mid-scene is what makes a character morph.
the craft

What actually makes it look like anime

🎯 Name the era, not the word "Anime" gets you generic. "Late-2000s psychological thriller, high contrast, desaturated palette, heavy shadow" gets you a look.
💡 Commit to one light source Say where it is in every shot. Inconsistent light is the fastest way to make cuts feel like different scenes.
📐 Vary shot size on purpose Anime leans on extreme wides for dread and extreme closes (just the eyes) for the turn. Alternating them is most of the drama.
⏸️ Use stillness Anime holds on a near-static frame with only hair or smoke moving. That's a feature, and it's exactly what these models do best.

3 bonus prompts to run next

1 · the consistency auditor 
Here are my shot prompts for this scene: [PASTE THEM]. Compare the character descriptions across every shot and flag any word that changed: hair, eye colour, outfit, age, palette, light direction. Give me a corrected set where every character block is identical, and tell me which shot was the odd one out.
2 · the 15-second problem solver 
My scene is [LENGTH] long but the video models in this flow cap at 15 seconds per clip. Break it into clips of 15 seconds or less, cutting on action or on a look so the joins feel intentional rather than like a limitation. For each clip, tell me the last frame, so I can use it as the first frame of the next one and keep it continuous.
3 · put me in it 
I'm attaching photos of my face. Turn me into an anime character in the style of [SERIES OR ERA], keeping my actual features recognisable: face shape, eye shape, hair. Give me a locked appearance block for my character, then a character sheet showing front, three-quarter and side view so I can reuse the same me in every future scene.
