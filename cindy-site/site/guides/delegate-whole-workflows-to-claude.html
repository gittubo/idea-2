the research

Why AI made people busier

Two researchers at UC Berkeley's Haas School of Business, Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye, followed 40 workers at a roughly 200-person US technology company from April to December 2025, across engineering, product, design, research and operations. They wrote it up in Harvard Business Review under the title AI Doesn't Reduce Work, It Intensifies It. Nobody's workload went down. Three things went up instead:

  • Pace. People worked faster, and faster became the new baseline expectation.
  • Scope. People took on a broader range of tasks, because AI made more things feel doable.
  • Hours. Work spread into breaks, evenings and early mornings, with fewer natural pauses.

The researchers' phrase for it is silent workload creep, and their warning was that it hides inside what looks like a productivity win. The Register's write-up has the study details outside the paywall if you want them.

Here is the part I care about: none of that is an argument against using AI. It is a description of what happens when you point AI at tasks instead of at processes. Speed up one task and you have not removed the work, you have raised the bar and kept the coordination, the checking and the deciding on your own plate.

The layer test, and it is the whole guide in one line. If finishing an AI task creates review work for you, you automated the wrong layer. Delegating "write me a draft" hands you an editing job. Delegating "run this process and hand me the finished thing, checked" hands you an outcome. The difference between those two is a written directive.
the deliverable

The directive template

A directive is a plain-English markdown file describing one whole process from start to finish. No code, no special syntax, and you write it once. It has seven sections, and the last two are the ones people leave out and then wonder why work keeps coming back.

📄 the directive template, copy this 
# [Name of the process]

## What this is for
One paragraph in plain English. What outcome this produces, who it is for, and when it should run. Write it so a competent stranger would understand what "finished" looks like before reading anything else.

## Inputs
Everything needed before starting, and exactly where each one lives. Name the folder, the file, the link, the inbox label. If an input is missing, stop and ask me rather than guessing or inventing a placeholder.

## Steps
1. The first thing to do, written as an instruction, not a description.
2. The next thing. One action per step.
3. Keep going until the process is genuinely finished, including the boring last mile: naming the file, putting it in the right folder, updating whatever tracks it.

## Rules and constraints
The non-negotiables. House style, tone, formats, banned words, things that must never happen, decisions that are mine and not yours. Anything I have corrected more than twice belongs here permanently.

## Definition of done
The checklist you run against your own output before telling me it is finished. Write it as things to verify, not things to do. If any check fails, fix it and re-run the checklist rather than reporting it to me.
- [ ] Check one, stated so it is objectively pass or fail
- [ ] Check two
- [ ] Check three

## What to do when you are stuck
The specific situations where you should stop and ask me instead of deciding: missing input, ambiguous requirement, anything that costs money, anything that gets sent to another person. For everything else, make a sensible call and note it.

## Output
Exactly what lands on my plate at the end, in what format, and where. One sentence. If I have to go looking for the result, this section is not finished.
Write it the way you would brief a capable new hire on their first Monday. That is the correct level of detail: not every keystroke, but every decision they could plausibly get wrong. The test is whether someone who has never done the job could follow it without messaging you.
see it real

A filled-in one

This is a genuinely small process, deliberately, because small ones are where you should start. Notice how much of it is the last three sections.

📄 example: weekly client update 
# Weekly client update

## What this is for
Produces the Friday client update email for each active client, ready for me to read and send. It should be sendable as written, not a draft I have to rewrite.

## Inputs
- The client folder in Drive, which holds the last four updates for tone and continuity.
- My notes file for the week, at notes/this-week.md.
- The shared task board, for what actually shipped and what slipped.
If the notes file is empty or the board has not moved, stop and ask me. Do not write an update from nothing.

## Steps
1. Read the last two updates for that client so the voice and the running threads carry over.
2. Pull what shipped this week, what moved, and what is blocked.
3. Write the update in this order: one-line summary, shipped, in progress, what I need from you, what is next.
4. Name anything that slipped honestly, with the new date, in one sentence and without an apology paragraph.
5. Save it as updates/YYYY-MM-DD-clientname.md and tell me the file path.

## Rules and constraints
- Under 250 words. Clients skim.
- No em dashes, no "we're excited to", no filler openers.
- Never promise a date I have not given you.
- Never mention another client by name.
- If a number is not in my notes or on the board, do not include it.

## Definition of done
- [ ] Every claim traces to the notes file or the task board, with nothing invented
- [ ] Under 250 words and in the five-section order above
- [ ] Every slipped item has a stated new date
- [ ] The "what I need from you" section has either a specific ask or the line "nothing this week"
- [ ] Saved with the correct filename and the path given to me

## What to do when you are stuck
Stop and ask if: the notes are missing, two sources disagree about status, or something looks like bad news the client has not heard yet. Everything else, decide and note the call at the bottom.

## Output
One markdown file per active client in updates/, plus a message listing the file paths and any decisions you made.
the section that matters

Definition of done, properly

This is the section that decides whether the work bounces back. Without it, Claude finishes when it runs out of instructions, which is not the same as finishing when the job is right. With it, Claude checks its own output and fixes what fails before you ever see it, which is exactly the review work you were trying to get off your plate.

The rule for writing one: every line has to be objectively pass or fail. "Make sure it is good" cannot be checked. "Every number appears in the source file" can.

Weak: "check the tone" Strong: "no sentence opens with 'I hope this finds you well', and nothing uses the words in my banned list."
Weak: "make it accurate" Strong: "every figure and date appears in the source file, quoted exactly, and nothing is estimated."
Weak: "keep it short" Strong: "under 250 words, counted, and no section runs longer than four lines."
Weak: "file it properly" Strong: "saved to the named folder with the date-first filename, and the tracker row updated."
If you can only add one thing to a process you already run, add this section. It is the cheapest change and it is the one that turns "here is a draft" into "here is the thing, done".
make it live

Install it, three ways

  1. 🌐 As a Claude Project, no terminal. On claude.ai, create a project for the process, paste the directive into the project instructions, and add the reference files it names to the project knowledge. Every chat you start inside that project runs with the directive already loaded, so you type "run the weekly client update" and nothing else. Anthropic's projects article covers the setup.
  2. 📦 As a skill, so it triggers by itself. Save the directive as a SKILL.md inside a folder named after the process, zip the folder, then in the Claude app open Customize, then Skills, then Add, then Upload a skill. Add a description line at the top saying when it should be used, and Claude reaches for it on its own when your request matches. This works on any plan, Free included, and the steps are in Anthropic's creating custom skills article.
  3. 💻 As a file in Claude Code. Keep a directives/ folder in the project, one markdown file per process, and reference it from your CLAUDE.md so Claude knows the folder exists. Then "run the weekly client update directive" is the whole instruction. This is the version I use, because the directive sits next to the files it operates on. The memory docs explain how CLAUDE.md is loaded, and slash commands let you give a directive its own shortcut.
Keep intent and implementation in separate files. The directive says what should happen in English. Any script it calls is a separate file that does one thing. Mixing the two produces a document nobody can edit later, including you.
start here

The prompt that writes your first one

You do not have to write the directive from a blank page. Have Claude interview you, because it will ask about the parts you have stopped noticing.

👩‍💻 draft my directive 
I want to hand you an entire process instead of asking you for one task at a time. Help me write a directive file for it, using this structure: what this is for, inputs, steps, rules and constraints, definition of done, what to do when stuck, and output.

The process is: [describe it roughly, the way you would explain it to a friend, including what triggers it and what you end up with].

Interview me before you write anything. Ask me, one small group at a time: where each input actually lives, the steps I do on autopilot that I have not mentioned, the corrections I find myself repeating, what makes a bad version bad, and which decisions must stay mine. Push back if an answer is vague, because vague answers become vague output.

Then write the directive in plain English. For the definition of done, every line must be objectively pass or fail, so rewrite any check that relies on judgement into something verifiable. Finish by telling me which parts you are least confident about and what you would need from me to tighten them.

Then run it once on real work, and every time something comes back wrong, add one line to the rules or the definition of done rather than re-explaining in chat. Three or four passes and the process stops needing you.

the check

4 signs you automated the wrong layer

You are editing every output The work moved from writing to reviewing, which is the same hour spent differently. The fix lives in the definition of done, not in a better prompt.
You are re-explaining context daily If you paste the same background every session, that background belongs in a file. You are paying the setup cost every single time.
The output stops one step early Claude wrote the thing but did not name it, file it, or update the tracker. That last mile belongs in the steps section, and it is where your time actually goes.
You got faster and busier The clearest signal of all, and the one the Berkeley study describes. More throughput with the same fatigue means you sped up tasks without ever removing a process.
