The creator-seat audit: 4 Claude prompts

why this one

The pattern behind the headlines

Amazon cut roughly 14,000 corporate roles in October 2025 and confirmed another 16,000 in January 2026, which is 30,000 in total and the largest workforce reduction in the company's history (GeekWire). Worth being accurate about the cause, because the internet was not: HR lead Beth Galetti pointed at AI as "the most transformative technology we've seen since the internet" while announcing the first round (NBC News), and Andy Jassy had already written to staff in June 2025 that "we will need fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today, and more people doing other types of jobs" (Amazon). Jassy also said publicly that the October round itself was about culture and layers rather than AI. Both things are on the record, and the second half of his sentence is the part nobody quoted.

That second half is the whole strategy. The work that gets absorbed first is the work that repeats a pattern someone has already seen. The work that does not is the work where you decide what the thing should be in the first place. This audit finds the line between those two inside your actual job, then moves you across it.

This is not a "quit and become a founder" guide. The creator seat exists inside a job: it is the person who decides what gets built, who defines the format, who owns the judgment call. You can move seats without changing employers.
the shape of it

The three moves

  1. 🔎 Audit. Paste a real week of work into Claude and have it sorted into pure repetition, repetition with judgment hidden inside, and genuine judgment or creation. You are looking for how many hours sit in the first bucket.
  2. 👩‍💻 Delegate. Take the top two or three repetitive tasks and turn each into a skill file, which is a small folder of instructions Claude loads when the task comes up, so it does the job your way instead of a generic way.
  3. 💡 Create. Spend the hours you free up on one thing that only you could have made, and make it visible. Hours saved quietly look like nothing. Hours saved and reinvested look like a promotion case.
prompt 1

The creator-seat audit

Open any Claude (the free plan is fine for this) and start a fresh chat. Before you paste, spend five minutes writing out a real week: every recurring task, roughly how long it takes, and how often it happens. Rough is fine, honest matters more than precise. Then paste this.

🔎 the creator-seat audit 
You are helping me run a creator-seat audit on my own work. My job is [job title] at [type of company], and here is an honest list of everything I actually did in a normal week, with rough time spent and how often each one recurs: [paste your list].

Sort every item into exactly one of four buckets and show it as a table with columns for task, hours per week, bucket, and a one-line reason. The buckets are: (1) pure repetition, where the same steps produce the same shape of output every time, (2) repetition with judgment inside, where the steps repeat but a human call is buried in the middle, (3) judgment, where the value is the decision rather than the output, and (4) creation, where I am defining something that did not exist before. Be blunt with me. If I have flattered a task by calling it strategic when it is really a template, say so and say why.

Then total the hours in each bucket and tell me what percentage of my week is currently spent in buckets one and two. Rank the bucket-one and bucket-two tasks by how much time I would get back per month if they were automated, and for the top three, describe in plain language what a good automated version would need to know about my format, my tone, and my inputs to produce work I would actually sign off on.

Finish with the honest part: name the two things in my week that are genuinely hard to replicate and explain what specifically makes them hard, whether that is taste, relationships, context nobody wrote down, or accountability for the outcome. Do not reassure me. If very little of my week sits in buckets three and four, tell me that plainly and tell me which single task is the most realistic bridge into creation work.
reading the answer

What each bucket actually means

1. Pure repetition Same input shape, same output shape, every time. Weekly reports, formatting, data pulls, standard replies. Delegate these first, they are the cleanest wins.
2. Repetition with judgment inside The steps repeat but one call in the middle is yours. Delegate the steps, keep the call. This is where a skill file with a "stop and ask me" instruction earns its keep.
3. Judgment The value is the decision, not the artefact. Prioritising, saying no, choosing between two credible options. Keep, and get faster at it by having Claude argue the other side.
4. Creation Defining a thing that did not exist: a new format, a new process, a new offer. This is the seat. If it is at zero hours, the rest of this guide is about buying you some.
Anthropic's own Economic Index tracks which tasks people actually hand to AI, and the pattern in the data matches the buckets: well-specified, repeatable tasks move first. Useful to skim before you decide your own list is unusual.
prompt 2

Turn the repetition into a skill file

A skill is a small folder with one SKILL.md file inside it: a name, a one-line description of when to use it, and your instructions. Claude reads that description and pulls the full skill in the moment your request matches, so you teach it your process once instead of re-explaining every week. No code involved, and it works on every plan including Free.

👩‍💻 build the skill for my top repetitive task 
Build me a reusable Claude skill for the task we ranked number one in my audit: [name the task, for example "the Monday client status report"]. I am not a developer, so write the whole thing for me and explain nothing in code terms.

Before you write it, ask me the four or five questions you genuinely need answered to get this right: what the inputs look like, what the finished output has to look like, what tone or house style it must match, what it must never do, and where a human decision has to stay with me. Wait for my answers.

Then write the SKILL.md. Give it a short lowercase hyphenated name that does not contain the words claude or anthropic, a description line that says both what it does and exactly when it should trigger, and numbered instructions specific enough that a competent stranger could follow them and produce the same result I would. Where my audit said a judgment call sits inside this task, write an explicit instruction to pause and ask me rather than guessing.

Finally, tell me how to save it as a zipped folder I can upload, and give me one realistic test case I can run on it this week to check whether the output is genuinely sign-off ready or only nearly there.
  1. 📄 Save what Claude gives you. You want a folder containing a SKILL.md file. Ask Claude to zip it if it has not already.
  2. ➕ Install it in the app. Open Claude, go to Customize, then Skills, click Add, and choose Upload a skill. Pick your zip. It is now available in every chat.
  3. 💻 Or drop it in Claude Code. If you use Claude Code, put the folder in your ~/.claude/skills/ directory instead. Official steps are in Anthropic's creating custom skills article.
  4. 🔁 Run it on a real task and correct it. Do not judge it on the first output. Run it, tell Claude precisely what was wrong, and ask it to update the SKILL.md. Two or three passes is normal and it is the whole trick.
  5. 📌 Repeat for tasks two and three. Three good skills covering your three most repetitive tasks is a realistic first month, and it is usually where the hours actually show up.
Keep the review step. A skill that drafts and hands you the draft is a genuine multiplier. A skill that sends, posts or files things without you reading them is how a small formatting error becomes a credibility problem. Draft mode first, always.
prompt 3

Decide what to make with the hours

This is the step people skip, and skipping it is why "I automated my reports" never turns into anything. Freed hours get quietly refilled with more admin unless you decide in advance what they are for.

💡 what to build with the freed hours 
I have freed up roughly [number] hours a week by automating repetitive parts of my job at [type of company], where I work as [job title]. I want to spend those hours on creation work: building something that did not exist before, that only I am well placed to build, and that makes my judgment visible to people who decide things.

First, ask me three questions about what my team or company is currently bad at, what problem I complain about most, and who would notice if it were fixed. Wait for my answers.

Then give me five candidate projects, each described in four lines: what it is, who it helps, why I am unusually well placed to build it given my role and access, and what the first visible version could look like within two weeks. Rank them by a simple test: how obvious would the value be to someone two levels above me who only sees the finished thing. Reject anything that requires permission I do not have or a budget I have to ask for.

For the top one, write me a two-week plan broken into six sessions of ninety minutes, each with a specific deliverable, and tell me the single most likely reason I will abandon it in week one and what to do instead when that happens.
prompt 4

The monthly re-run

Repetition creeps back. New tools, new processes, a new manager with a new report format, and six months later your week has quietly refilled. Put this one in your calendar on the first Monday of the month.

🔁 the monthly creator-seat check 
We ran a creator-seat audit on my work previously. Here is what my week looks like now: [paste your current recurring tasks with rough hours]. Here are the skills or automations I already have running: [list them].

Re-sort my week into the same four buckets: pure repetition, repetition with judgment inside, judgment, and creation. Compare the percentages to last time if I have given you the earlier numbers, and tell me plainly whether I moved toward creation work or away from it.

Then do three things. Name any new repetitive task that has crept in since last time and is now worth automating. Name any existing skill of mine that is probably underperforming, based on tasks I am clearly still doing by hand, and tell me what to fix in its instructions. And name one thing I am still doing manually out of habit rather than necessity, where the real reason is that handing it over feels uncomfortable rather than risky.

End with one sentence I could actually say in a performance review that describes what I created this month, not what I maintained.
the honest bit

Where this framing breaks

Two things worth holding lightly. The first is that "AI only remixes, humans create" is a comforting line and it is not a law of physics, so build your position on being the person who decides and is accountable, not on a capability gap that may narrow.

The second is a good reminder about how much invisible human work sits behind polished automation. Reporting in 2024 said around a thousand workers in India were involved in reviewing and annotating footage behind Amazon's Just Walk Out checkout (Business Standard). Amazon disputed the framing, saying associates annotate video to improve the model rather than watching live shoppers, and published its own account when it moved Fresh stores to smart carts (Amazon). Both versions point the same way for you: new systems still need humans in the loop, and being one of the people the system depends on is a better position than being one of the steps it replaces.

If your audit comes back with almost everything in buckets one and two, that is information, not a verdict. Pick the single task in bucket two with the most judgment buried inside it, get very good at that part specifically, and build your first creation project next to it. That is the realistic bridge, and it is a much better use of the next quarter than panic.
