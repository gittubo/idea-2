The 4-prompt pressure test for Claude

💡 Why four and not one. Each prompt attacks a different failure. One asks how sure the model is, one hunts for the ways it breaks, one changes whose head it's answering from, and the last one gets you out of the chat entirely and into something you can check. Ask only for "are you sure?" and you get reassurance, which is the least useful answer available.
before you paste anything

How to run it

The order matters, and so does the pacing. Each answer becomes context for the next prompt, so Claude is critiquing progressively more of its own thinking as you go. Paste them all at once and you get one blended, watered-down response instead of four distinct passes.

  1. 🎯 Start with a real answer. Ask your actual question first and let Claude answer normally. The pressure test runs on top of an existing answer, it isn't something you bolt onto the original question.
  2. 1️⃣ Paste prompt one, wait, read it. Actually read the confidence breakdown before moving on. Half the time the useful information is right there, in the one claim it quietly scored a 4.
  3. 2️⃣ Then two, then three, then four. One at a time, in the same chat, no other messages in between. Anything you type in between becomes context and softens the critique.
  4. 📝 Keep a running note of what changed. The gap between the first answer and the fourth is the part you nearly acted on. That gap is the whole point of the exercise.
📌 Do not soften the prompts. Adding "if you don't mind" or "sorry to push" gives Claude an easy exit back to agreeing with itself. These are written blunt on purpose.

Prompt 1: score your own confidence

A first answer arrives with no signal about which parts are solid and which parts got filled in. This prompt forces the answer to be broken into separate claims with a number attached to each, so you can see the soft bits.

It works better than it sounds like it should. Stanford researchers found that when models fine-tuned with human feedback (ChatGPT, GPT-4 and Claude among them) are asked to say their confidence out loud, those spoken confidence scores are "typically better-calibrated than the model's conditional probabilities", often cutting calibration error by around half (Tian et al., Just Ask for Calibration). Asking is genuinely informative, as long as you ask per claim rather than for the answer as a whole.

1️⃣ how sure are you, really 
Before I act on this, rate your confidence in the answer you just gave me out of 10, and explain the number.

Break your answer into its separate claims and give each claim its own score, because an answer is almost always a mix of things you are certain about and things you filled in to sound complete. For anything you score 7 or below, say plainly what you are unsure about and why it is shaky. Then tell me exactly what would raise each score: which detail from me you are missing, or which source you would need to check. Do not rewrite the answer yet, I only want the honest read on it.

Prompt 2: what could fall apart

The first answer is built for the situation Claude assumed you were in. This prompt hunts for the version of your situation where that answer quietly becomes the wrong advice, which is the failure that actually costs you something.

2️⃣ argue against your own answer 
Now argue against your own answer. What could fall apart here, and what is the version of this answer that turns out to be wrong?

Give me specific failure cases rather than general caution: the assumptions you made about my situation that might not hold, the edge cases you skipped over, and the exact conditions under which your advice becomes the wrong advice. For each one, tell me how I would notice it happening early, while it is still cheap to fix. Then name the single most likely failure of the set, and tell me what you would change in the answer to protect against that one.
🌱 If the reply comes back as a list of generic risks ("results may vary", "consider consulting a professional"), reply with: "none of those are specific to what I told you, redo it using only details from my situation." That one line usually turns the whole thing around.

Prompt 3: answer again as the expert

A general answer is built from what is written down most often, which is rarely what a practitioner would actually do. Naming a specific role, with a specific amount of hands-on experience, pulls a different set of considerations into the answer: the standard practice, the thing that always goes wrong in real cases, the shortcut nobody bothers to write down.

3️⃣ redo it with expertise in front 
Answer this again from scratch as a [role, for example: senior tax accountant who has filed hundreds of these / performance marketer who has spent seven figures on Meta / employment lawyer who negotiates these contracts weekly], and start by telling me what your first answer missed.

Do not politely restate the earlier answer, redo the thinking with that expertise in front of it. Name the things a practitioner would weigh that a general answer would not: the standard practice in this field, the thing that always goes wrong in real cases, and the shortcut people who do this every week actually use. Then give me a short list of what changed between your two answers, and say which version you would stake your professional reputation on and why.
📌 Be specific with the role. "Act as an expert" changes almost nothing. "Act as a senior tax accountant who has filed hundreds of these" changes the entire answer, because it names the experience you want the answer filtered through.

Prompt 4: hand me a way to check it

This is the one that earns the whole routine, and it's the one people skip. The first three prompts are Claude checking Claude, and there's a real ceiling on that: a widely cited ICLR 2024 paper found that "LLMs struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback, and at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction" (Huang et al., Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet). So the fourth prompt stops asking the model to grade itself and asks it to hand you the external check instead.

4️⃣ how do I verify this without you 
Now hand me a way to check this without you. How would I verify each important claim in your answer myself?

For every factual claim, give me the primary source I should look at (the specific document, the official body, the page or section, or the exact search phrase that finds it), plus what that source needs to say for your claim to hold up. Where something cannot be sourced, label it clearly as your judgement rather than fact instead of leaving me to guess which is which. Finish with the three fastest checks that would catch the biggest error if you are wrong, ordered by what I should do first.
⚠️ Open the sources. Fabricated citations are a real and expensive failure mode: Stanford HAI benchmarked the paid legal research tools built specifically to prevent it and found Lexis+ AI and Ask Practical Law AI "produced incorrect information more than 17% of the time", with Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research over 34% (Stanford HAI, AI on Trial). A link you did not click is not a source.

Four prompts, four different mistakes

They are not four ways of asking the same thing, which is why stacking them works and why dropping one leaves a hole.

1. Overconfidence Catches the claim that got filled in to make the answer feel complete. You see the soft parts as numbers instead of guessing at them.
2. Missing edge cases Catches the answer that is right in general and wrong for you, because of an assumption about your situation you never got told about.
3. Shallow expertise Catches the textbook answer. A practitioner's version surfaces the standard practice and the thing that always goes wrong in real cases.
4. Unverifiable claims Catches invented facts and dead citations, and it's the only one of the four that gets you outside the model to something you can independently check.
be honest about it

When to run it, and when not to

Running four extra prompts on every message would be exhausting and mostly wasted. The test is for answers you're about to act on.

✅ Worth the two minutes Anything with money, legal, tax, health, or contract consequences. Anything going to a client, your boss, or a public audience. Any number you're about to put in a deck or a decision.
🌱 Skip it Drafting, brainstorming, rewriting, formatting, naming things, tidying a message. Work where being wrong costs you a redo, not a real outcome.
💡 A shortcut for the in-between cases: run prompt 1 on its own. If everything comes back an 8 or above with reasons attached, carry on. If anything lands at 6, run the other three on that specific claim rather than the whole answer.

Turn it into a /pressure skill

Once you've run this a few times, stop pasting it. A skill is a small folder with a SKILL.md inside that holds a name, a description of when to use it, and your instructions. Claude reads the description automatically and pulls in the full skill when your request matches, so the routine becomes one word. Skills work on every plan, Free through Enterprise, and you don't write any code.

⚡ build the pressure test skill 
Build me a reusable Claude skill called "pressure" that runs a four-pass pressure test on an answer you have just given me.

Use skill-creator to write it. The SKILL.md description should say to use it whenever I type /pressure or ask you to pressure test, sanity check or stress test an answer. The instructions should run four passes in order and label each one clearly in your reply: pass one scores your confidence per individual claim out of 10 and names what would raise each score, pass two argues against your own answer with specific failure cases and early warning signs, pass three redoes the answer from scratch as a named expert practitioner and lists what changed, and pass four gives me primary sources I can check myself plus the three fastest checks that would catch the biggest error. Finish every run with a short "what I would change" summary and a plain flag on anything I should not act on yet. Then package it as a zipped folder I can upload.
  1. 📦 Take the folder it hands you. skill-creator finishes with a skill folder containing a SKILL.md. That folder is the skill, there is nothing else to build.
  2. ➕ Add it to Claude. In the app, open Customize, then Skills, click Add, and choose upload a skill to add the zip. In Claude Code, drop the folder into ~/.claude/skills/ instead. Anthropic's create custom skills article has the same steps with screenshots.
  3. 🔁 Test it on an answer you already know is shaky. Run it against something you've already checked by hand, so you can see whether it catches what you caught. Tell it what it missed and have it edit the SKILL.md.
  4. ⚡ Use it. Type /pressure after any answer that matters. Four passes, one word, no pasting.
🌱 Worth adding to your SKILL.md once you know your own work: the domains you care about (your industry, your country's rules, your platform) so pass three always picks a relevant expert instead of a generic one.
