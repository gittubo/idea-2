Read this before you build anything. In July 2025 YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" policy to inauthentic content, and mass-produced, templated, low-effort videos are not eligible for ad revenue. A robotic voice read over stock clips with nothing original added is exactly what that policy targets. AI is completely allowed. What is not allowed is churning out the same template with no original insight. Everything in this guide is built so your channel lands on the right side of that line, which is why the script system and your own point of view do the heavy lifting, not the automation.
the shape of it

The four moving parts

A faceless channel sounds like a lot until you see it as four jobs. Claude does two of them, one tool does the voice, one tool does the assembly. That is the entire pipeline.

  1. 🎯 The niche decision. Claude scores candidate niches on how repeatable the content is, how big the search demand is, and whether there is money in it beyond ad revenue. This is the decision that quietly sets your ceiling.
  2. 🧠 The script system. Not a prompt you retype every week. You write the rules once (hook shape, open loops, spoken English, visual tags) and save them, so every future script comes out in the same structure.
  3. 🎙️ The voice. ElevenLabs turns the script into narration. There is a real plan gate here that most videos skip over, and it matters if you plan to monetize.
  4. 🎞️ The assembly. CapCut on the timeline, free Pexels footage for the visuals, and Claude writing the title, description, tags and chapter markers.
step one

Let Claude pick the niche

Most faceless channels die because someone picked a niche they found interesting instead of one that pays and refills forever. Ask Claude to score options against those two things and argue with you about it.

🎯 the niche scorecard 
I'm starting a faceless YouTube channel. I will not appear on camera, the narration will be AI voice, and I can realistically spend about 3 to 5 hours a week on it. Here is what I actually know something about or find genuinely interesting: [list 4 to 6 topics, be honest and specific].

For each one, score it out of 10 on four things and explain your score in one or two sentences: (1) content repeatability, meaning can I make 100 videos on this without repeating myself, (2) search demand, meaning are people actively looking this up rather than only being fed it, (3) monetization beyond ad revenue, meaning affiliate products, digital products, sponsors or services that fit naturally, and (4) how crowded it already is and whether a new channel can realistically get a foothold.

Then rank them, tell me which single one you would commit to and why, and name the one you think I am emotionally attached to but should not pick. Finish with 15 specific video titles for your top pick that a real person would search for, and flag which three of those you would film first.
On the RPM numbers you see everywhere. YouTube does not publish revenue per mille (RPM) by niche, so every "finance pays $20 per thousand views" figure floating around is a creator estimate, not an official number. What is true is that RPM varies enormously by topic and by where your viewers live, because advertisers bid more for some audiences. YouTube explains how RPM is actually calculated here. Once you have any views at all, your own real number lives in YouTube Studio under Analytics, then Revenue. Treat that as the only figure worth planning around.
step two

Build the script system, not a prompt

This is the part that separates a channel that lasts from a folder of half-finished scripts. A one-off prompt gives you a one-off script and a different structure every time. A system gives you the same reliable structure on video 40 as on video 4, which is what makes batching possible.

You have two ways to save it, and both work on a free Claude account.

  1. 📁 The Project route (easiest). In Claude, create a new Project for your channel and paste the rules below into its instructions. Every chat you start inside that Project inherits them automatically. This is the one I would start with.
  2. 🧩 The skill route (more portable). Package the same rules as a skill so you can trigger them from any chat with one phrase. Skills are available on every plan, Free through Enterprise. In the Claude app go to Customize, then Skills, then Add, then Upload a skill. Anthropic's official walkthrough is here, and the full explanation of what a skill is lives in the Agent Skills overview.

Either way, this is the text that becomes your system. Paste it as your Project instructions, or hand it to Claude and ask it to package it as a skill.

🧠 the script system rules 
You are my scriptwriter for a faceless YouTube channel about [your niche]. Every script you write for me follows these rules without me having to restate them.

Structure: open with a pattern interrupt in the first 15 seconds that contradicts what the viewer expects, then state plainly what they will know by the end. Plant an open loop in the first 30 seconds that you only pay off in the final third, and plant a second one at the midpoint so there is always a reason to keep watching. Deliver the value in clearly separated beats, and give each beat one idea, not three.

Voice: write spoken English, not essay English. Short sentences. Contractions. Say numbers the way a person says them out loud. No corporate filler, no "in today's video", no "let's dive in", no em dashes. If a sentence would be awkward to read aloud in one breath, split it.

Formatting: mark every natural pause with [pause] so the voiceover has rhythm. Every 15 to 25 seconds, add a visual tag on its own line in the form [VISUAL: what should be on screen right now], and be concrete enough that I could search a stock library for it. End with a soft call to action that fits the video's topic, never a generic "smash subscribe".

Before you write, ask me the three details you need most about the specific video. After you write, list every claim in the script that needs a source, and tell me the weakest 15 seconds and how you would fix it.
The reason this works is that the rules stop being your job. You stop reviewing structure on every script and start reviewing only the thing that actually needs a human: whether the idea is any good and whether the take is genuinely yours.

Once the system is saved, writing a video is one line.

✍️ write video number 7 
Write the script for this video: [your title]. Target length is [8] minutes spoken, which is roughly [1,100] words at a natural pace.

Here is what I want the viewer to walk away believing, in my own words: [two or three sentences of your actual opinion, not a summary of the topic]. Use that as the spine of the video and build the beats around it rather than writing a neutral overview.

Follow my script system exactly. When you are done, give me the three strongest alternative hooks for the first 15 seconds so I can pick, and tell me honestly whether this idea is strong enough to be worth filming or whether it is a section of a better video.
step three

The voice, and the plan gate nobody mentions

ElevenLabs turns the script into narration. Setup is genuinely quick: create an account, open the text to speech tool, pick a voice from the library, paste your script, generate, then download the MP3. The part worth knowing before you build a business on it is the licensing.

The free tier does not carry a commercial licence. ElevenLabs' free plan gives you 10,000 credits a month, which is enough to test voices and hear your script out loud, but the commercial licence starts on the paid Starter tier at $6 a month, which also unlocks instant voice cloning. If your channel is monetized, or you ever plan to monetize it, you want the licence sorted before you upload, not after. Current plans and inclusions are on the ElevenLabs pricing page.
  1. 🔊 Audition three voices, not one. Paste the same 60 seconds of your real script into three different library voices and listen back on phone speakers, because that is where most of your audience will hear it. The voice that sounds best in headphones is often not the one that survives a phone speaker.
  2. ⏸️ Feed it your pause markers. Those [pause] tags from the script system are there for a reason. Replace them with a line break or a comma before you generate, and the delivery stops sounding like a robot reading a paragraph.
  3. 🎚️ Lock one voice and keep it. Channel identity is mostly voice when there is no face. Changing narrator between videos quietly resets the familiarity you are trying to build.
  4. 💾 Save the settings you landed on. Write down the voice name, stability and similarity settings in your channel doc. Video 30 should sound like video 3.
step four

Visuals without a camera

Your visual tags did the hard thinking already. Now it is sourcing and assembly, and both parts are free.

  1. 🔎 Pull the shot list out of the script. Every [VISUAL: ...] line in your script is a search term. Copy them into a list and work down it, so you are never sitting in a stock library wondering what you need.
  2. 🎥 Download from Pexels. Free photos and video, and the Pexels licence allows commercial use with attribution optional. The restrictions worth knowing: do not show identifiable people in a negative or offensive context, do not imply a person or brand endorses you, do not resell the files unmodified, and do not upload them to a competing stock site.
  3. ✂️ Assemble in CapCut. Drop the ElevenLabs MP3 on the timeline first, then lay clips against it so the visuals follow the narration instead of the other way around. CapCut is free on desktop and mobile and does captions, transitions and text overlays without a learning curve.
  4. 🖼️ Design the thumbnail before you upload. Ask Claude for six thumbnail concepts (three to five words of on-screen text plus the image idea) for your title, then build the winner in whatever design tool you already use. A great video with a flat thumbnail does not get the chance to be great.
Add something only you can add. A screen recording of you doing the thing, a chart you built from real numbers, your own annotated screenshots. Two or three original elements per video is the difference between a channel with a point of view and a template, and it is also what keeps you clear of the inauthentic content policy above.
step five

Let Claude write the metadata

Titles, description, tags and chapters are the unglamorous part people rush at 11pm. Hand the finished script back to Claude and get all of it in one pass.

📝 the full metadata pack 
Here is the finished script for my next video: [paste the script]. My channel is about [niche] and my viewer is [describe them in one sentence].

Give me all of the following. First, 10 title options, mixed between curiosity-driven and search-driven, each under 60 characters, and tell me which single one you would publish and why. Second, a description where the first two lines work as a standalone hook because that is all anyone sees before "show more", followed by a short summary, then chapter timestamps pulled from the actual beats of the script.

Third, 15 tags ordered from most specific to most general. Fourth, three pinned-comment options that give the viewer a genuine reason to reply rather than begging for engagement. Keep everything in plain spoken English with no em dashes and no corporate filler.
Before you commit to a title, sanity-check the demand with a free keyword tool. vidIQ and TubeBuddy both have free browser-extension tiers that show you search volume and competition on the terms you are considering. You are looking for a topic people search for that does not already have five polished videos on page one.
the cadence

How the week actually runs

The system only pays off if you batch. Doing one video end to end, one at a time, is how faceless channels quietly stop.

Monday, one hour Pick the week's topics from your title list and write all the scripts in one sitting inside your Project. The system makes this the fastest hour of the week.
Tuesday, 45 minutes Generate every voiceover in one go, save them all in one folder, named to match the scripts so nothing gets mixed up.
Wednesday, two hours Assemble in CapCut back to back. Batching the edit means you stay in one headspace instead of re-learning your own timeline every time.
Thursday, 30 minutes Metadata pack, thumbnails, and schedule the uploads. Then you are done, and next week starts from a full title list.
A realistic starting pace. One to two videos in your first week while you find your rhythm, then three to four a week once the batch runs smoothly. And on expectations: ad revenue only switches on once you are in the YouTube Partner Program, which currently needs 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 valid public watch hours in the past 12 months or 10 million valid Shorts views in the past 90 days, per YouTube's own requirements. That is months of consistent posting for most channels, so build the affiliate or product angle your niche prompt surfaced rather than waiting on AdSense alone.
stay legitimate

Four rules that keep the channel monetizable

Have a take Every video carries an opinion, a test you ran, or an angle that is yours. Original perspective is the specific thing YouTube's policy asks for.
Disclose synthetic content YouTube asks you to flag realistic AI-generated content in the upload flow. The rules are here, and disclosing costs you nothing.
Vary the format Identical structure, identical visuals and identical pacing across 50 uploads reads as mass production. Let the format breathe.
Check your facts Your script system already lists the claims that need a source. Open them and verify them before recording, every time.
