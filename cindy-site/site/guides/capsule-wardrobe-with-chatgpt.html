Turn your closet into a capsule wardrobe with ChatGPT

the afternoon plan

What you are doing, in four moves

1. Shoot Photograph your clothes in batches on a plain surface, so every piece is visible and its colour reads correctly.
2. Catalogue Have ChatGPT list every item back to you with colour, formality and season, and correct anything it misreads.
3. Capsule Ask it to pick the colour-coordinated core, then list every outfit those pieces make.
4. Keep it Save the capsule somewhere that remembers, because a single chat will not hold your closet forever.
move one

Photograph your clothes properly

This is the step that decides whether the rest is useful. A model reading a heap of clothes on a chair gives you heap-quality advice. Ten minutes of laying things out changes the whole output.

  1. 🌱 Work in batches, not one photo per item. Group by category: tops, bottoms, dresses, outerwear and layers, shoes, bags and belts. Six to ten photos total is plenty for most wardrobes.
  2. 💡 Lay flat on a plain surface. A made bed, a wooden floor, anything one colour. Space the pieces so nothing overlaps, because overlapping garments get merged into one item.
  3. 🧠 Shoot in daylight, near a window, with the flash off. Colour is the entire basis of a capsule, and warm indoor bulbs turn a cool grey into a beige. If a colour is unusual, say so in text when you upload.
  4. 👩‍💻 Include the boring pieces. Plain tees, the black trousers, the trainers you wear constantly. Basics are usually the hardest-working items in the whole wardrobe, and leaving them out skews the capsule towards clothes you rarely reach for.
  5. 🌱 Skip anything you would not wear this season. Do not photograph what does not fit, needs repair, or you have not worn in a year. Sorting that pile is a different afternoon.
💡 Keep every photo in one album on your phone. You will re-upload the same set later when you try a different app or start a new chat, and re-shooting is what stops most people doing this twice.

The prompts, in order

Run these in one chat, in sequence. Each one builds on the answer before it, so context accumulates instead of resetting.

1. The inventory. Upload your photos, then paste this. Do not skip to the capsule, because a catalogue you have corrected is what makes every later answer accurate.

👩‍💻 catalogue my closet 
These photos are my entire current-season wardrobe, laid out in batches by category. Go through them and build me a numbered inventory of every separate item you can see.

For each item give me: a short name I would recognise it by, the category (top, bottom, dress, outer layer, shoe, accessory), the colour described precisely rather than loosely (for example warm cream, cool charcoal, mid-wash denim), whether it reads as a neutral or an accent colour, the formality on a scale of 1 to 5 where 1 is loungewear and 5 is formal, and the seasons it works in.

Where you are unsure of a colour or a fabric, say so and ask me rather than guessing, because I will be building a colour-coordinated capsule on top of this list. At the end, tell me how many items you counted in each category and flag any category that looks thin.

Read the inventory and correct it in plain language before continuing: "item 14 is olive, not brown", "the third top is silk, so it is a 4 not a 2". Two minutes here saves every prompt after it.

2. The capsule. This is the one that does the actual work.

🌱 build my capsule 
Using the corrected inventory, build me a colour-coordinated capsule wardrobe from what I already own. Do not suggest anything I do not have yet in this answer.

Pick the core pieces that work hardest together and explain the colour logic in one short paragraph: which two or three neutrals are carrying the capsule, which accent colours sit against them, and which of my items clash with that core and should sit outside the capsule for now. Then list every outfit combination the capsule makes, numbered, each written as the actual items by name (top plus bottom plus layer plus shoes), and tag each outfit with the occasion it suits: casual day, work, dinner or going out, and travel.

Group the outfits so I can see them by occasion, and tell me the total number of outfits the capsule produces. If a piece only appears in one outfit, call it out, because that tells me it is not earning its place. My climate is [your city or typical weather] and my usual week looks like [for example: three days at a desk, gym most evenings, one dinner out].

3. The hardest-working pieces and the real gaps. This is where the shopping list stops being a wish list.

🧠 what earns its place, and what is missing 
Now analyse the capsule you just built. Rank my top ten hardest-working pieces by how many different outfits each one appears in, and show the count next to each, so I can see which items are actually carrying my wardrobe.

Then find the gaps, but only the gaps that unlock real outfits. For each suggested purchase tell me: exactly what it is including the colour, how many new outfit combinations it would create with pieces I already own, which specific existing items it pairs with, and a realistic price range. Rank the suggestions by outfits unlocked per dollar, and cap the list at five things.

Be honest with me: if a gap is a nice-to-have rather than something that unlocks outfits, say so and put it at the bottom. Also name anything in my wardrobe that appears in zero outfits, and tell me whether it is worth keeping for a specific occasion or whether it is genuinely dead weight.

4. The lookbook. Ask for the visual version so you can see the combinations rather than reading them.

💡 make me a lookbook grid 
Create a clean lookbook image of my capsule outfits, laid out as a grid with one outfit per cell, flat-lay style on a plain cream background, with each garment drawn in the exact colours from my inventory and the outfit number labelled underneath.

Start with the first eight outfits from the casual day and work groups. Keep the illustration simple and consistent: same angle, same spacing, no models, no busy backgrounds, no text other than the outfit numbers.

Then, separately from the image, give me the same eight outfits as a plain text list I can paste into my notes app, each one written as the item names plus the occasion, so I have a version I can actually search on my phone.
💡 Honest expectation on the image: an image model draws an approximation of your clothes, it does not collage your real photographs, so treat the grid as a visual reference rather than a catalogue of your actual items. The text list is the part you will use daily. Image generation is also rate-limited on free accounts, so if it stops, ask for four outfits at a time instead of eight.

5. The week planner. The prompt that turns the capsule into something you use on a Tuesday morning.

🌱 dress me for the week 
Using only the capsule outfits you built, plan my outfits for the next seven days. My week is [list what you are doing each day, for example: Monday office, Tuesday work from home, Wednesday office plus dinner, Thursday gym and errands, Friday office, Saturday brunch, Sunday nothing].

For each day give me one outfit by item name, one alternative in case something is in the wash, and a one-line reason it suits the day. Do not repeat the same top two days running, and spread the wear across the wardrobe rather than leaning on the same three favourites.

At the end, tell me which items I will need to wash mid-week for this plan to work, and which two pieces I did not use at all this week.
the caveat

A chat will not remember your closet

The inventory lives inside that conversation. Start a new chat next month and you are uploading everything again. Two ways to keep it, and they solve different halves of the problem.

  1. 🧠 Keep it inside ChatGPT with a project. Create a project in the ChatGPT sidebar, name it something like Wardrobe, and keep every wardrobe chat inside it. Add your corrected inventory and the capsule list as files or notes in the project so new chats start from them. This keeps the reasoning, which is the part you actually want back.
  2. 👩‍💻 Keep the visuals in a wardrobe app. A closet app stores the photographed items with backgrounds removed, so you can see and sort your real clothes. This keeps the pictures, which a chat cannot do well.

Three wardrobe apps worth the upload, and what each is actually good at:

  • Whering is free, on iOS and Android, and removes photo backgrounds for you as you add items. It also lets you add pieces by searching a very large catalogue of existing products rather than photographing them, plans outfits ahead, and tracks cost per wear. Best first choice if you want the full digital closet without paying anything.
  • Cladwell is built specifically around capsules, which makes it the closest match to what you have made here. It has a free tier for basic closet management, with paid plans starting under five dollars a month, daily outfit suggestions based on your local weather, editable capsule templates, and cost-per-wear analytics.
  • Vesta is iOS only and freemium, with an optional Pro subscription. It learns your preferences over time and suggests daily outfits using the weather and what is in your calendar, so it leans more towards styling you each morning than towards cataloguing.
🌱 Pick one and load the same photo album you already shot. The upload is the only real work, and you have already done the hard part by laying everything out.
worth knowing

Four things that make the answers better

Correct it out loud Every wrong colour or fabric you fix early compounds through the capsule, the gaps and the week plan. Treat the inventory as a draft, not an answer.
Give it your real life Climate, commute, dress code, how often you do laundry. A capsule built for a life you do not live is the reason generic wardrobe advice never sticks.
Ask why, not only what "Explain why these three neutrals work together" teaches you the rule, so the next purchase you make on your own is still on-plan.
Re-run it seasonally Photograph again when the weather turns and rerun the same prompts. Twenty minutes, twice a year, and the shopping list stays short.
