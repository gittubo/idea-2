before you start

What the data actually says

The number worth knowing is narrower than the headlines. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, working from real payroll data, found employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations sits about 19% below where it would otherwise be. Experienced workers in those same occupations show no comparable gap, and the mechanism is slower hiring rather than layoffs.

The part that matters more for you is the sorting rule underneath it. Declines cluster where AI substitutes for the human task. Where AI complements the person, employment is flat or rising. So the useful question is not "is my job on a list", it is "which of my tasks can be handed in finished by a model, and which ones need me in the loop". These four builds answer that, then move your week toward the second kind.

🌱 The thesis in one line. If AI can finish your task, change the task. If it multiplies your judgment, double down on it. Everything below is a way of doing one of those two things on purpose.
the plan

The four builds, ranked

Ordered fastest first, so the quick one tells you where to spend the bigger effort.

  1. 🧠 The task audit (about 30 minutes). Paste a real week of work into Claude and have it sort every task into delegate, do together, and human-only. Mechanism: you stop guessing which parts of your role are exposed and get a sorted list with hours attached, so the next three builds aim at something real.
  2. 👩‍💻 Your SKILL.md (about 30 to 45 minutes). Package the thing you are genuinely best at into a skill file Claude loads automatically. Mechanism: your unwritten method becomes a portable asset with your name on it, instead of knowledge that only exists while you are in the room.
  3. 📊 One visible tool (about 30 to 60 minutes). Turn a spreadsheet your team already argues over into a one-page dashboard and share it the same day. Mechanism: leverage that people can open and click is evidence. Quiet competence is not visible when decisions get made.
  4. 🗂️ Own the workflow (about 45 to 60 minutes). Rebuild a recurring deliverable as a Claude Project so it runs in minutes instead of hours. Mechanism: the person who operates the automated version of a process cannot be swapped out by that process. They run it.
build one

The task audit

This is the one to do today, even if you do nothing else. It takes a vague worry and turns it into a list with hours next to it. Open any Claude chat, paste last week's calendar and task list, and run this.

📋 the task audit prompt 
I want to audit my work week and see which parts of my job you can already
do, which parts I should be doing WITH you, and which parts are human-only.

My role: [job title, industry, team size, who I report to].
My week, pasted from my calendar and task list: [paste the real thing here].

Sort every single task into three buckets. Bucket 1, delegate now: you could
produce a usable first version from inputs I already have. Bucket 2, do
together: you draft or analyse, I direct, verify and decide. Bucket 3,
human-only: it runs on relationships, judgment, taste or accountability that
I hold. For every bucket 1 task, tell me exactly which inputs you would need
from me and estimate how long that task takes me today.

Then total the hours sitting in bucket 1 and give me a redeployment plan.
Pick the single task I should automate first, choosing the best ratio of
hours saved to effort to set up, and explain why you picked it. Say which
bucket 3 work I should move the recovered hours into so my week shifts
toward judgment instead of drafting. Finish with one honest sentence I could
say in a performance review about that shift.
💡 Read the output like this. A long bucket 1 is not bad news, it is your build list. A thin bucket 3 is the real signal: it means your week is mostly drafting, and that is the thing to change first. Ask Claude a follow-up if bucket 3 looks empty: "what bucket 3 work exists in my role that I am not currently doing, and how would I earn my way into it?"

Whatever comes out on top of that redeployment plan is what you build in the next three sections.

build two

Your SKILL.md

A skill is a small folder with one markdown file inside it. It holds a name, a one-line description of when to use it, and your steps. Claude reads that description on its own and pulls in the whole skill the moment a request matches, so you explain your method once and it runs your way in every conversation after that. The mechanism here is ownership: a generic model has no access to how your team actually does the work until somebody writes it down, and that somebody should be you.

  1. 🎯 Pick the task you are best at, not the one you hate most. The skill should encode judgment other people ask you for. The weekly report, the client email that lands, the way you turn messy notes into decisions.
  2. 📄 Fill in the starter below. Save it as a file called SKILL.md inside a folder named after the skill. The folder plus that one file is the whole thing.
  3. 📦 Zip the folder. Right click the folder, compress it. That zip is what you upload.
  4. ➕ Add it to Claude. In the Claude app, open Customize, then Skills, then Add, then Upload a skill and pick your zip. In Claude Code, drop the folder into ~/.claude/skills/ instead. Either way it is now live.
  5. 🔁 Run it on real work twice. Read what comes back, tell Claude what is off, and have it rewrite the SKILL.md. Two passes is usually the difference between "close" and "this is exactly how I do it".

Here is the starter. Everything in square brackets is yours to replace, and the rest is the structure that makes it work. Two formatting rules for the top block: the name must be lowercase and hyphenated, under 64 characters, with no "claude" or "anthropic" in it or the upload gets rejected, and the description has to fit in 200 characters.

📄 SKILL.md starter, fill in the blanks 
---
name: your-skill-name-here
description: Replace this line with what the skill does AND when to use it, in one sentence.
---

# [The task, in plain words]

## When to use this
Use this whenever [the trigger, e.g. I paste raw notes and ask for the
Monday status report].

## What I will give you
- [input 1, e.g. my raw notes from the week]
- [input 2, e.g. last week's version, for continuity]
- [input 3, e.g. the metrics export]

## Steps
1. [the first thing you always do, e.g. pull the wins, blockers and next
   steps out of the raw notes]
2. [the checking step, e.g. verify every number against the metrics export
   and drop anything unsupported]
3. [the formatting step, e.g. write it in our structure: one-line summary,
   then Wins, Blockers, Next]
4. [the finishing step, e.g. list anything needing a decision from my
   manager separately at the bottom]

## Rules that matter
- [non-negotiable 1, e.g. never invent a number, ask me instead]
- [non-negotiable 2, e.g. keep it under 250 words]
- [non-negotiable 3, e.g. plain language, no buzzwords, no filler]

## Worked example
Input: [paste a short, real example of what I hand over]
Output: [paste the finished version I was happy with]
🌱 The description line does the heavy lifting. It decides whether Claude ever picks your skill up. Weak: "writes reports." Strong: "Writes our Monday client status report in the team format. Use when I paste raw notes or a task export and ask for the weekly report." Be specific about the trigger, not the outcome.
💡 Do not want to write it by hand? Paste the starter into Claude with this: "Here is a SKILL.md template. Interview me with four questions about my format, my inputs and my tone, then fill it in for me and package it as a zipped folder I can upload." You answer four questions and it hands you the finished file.

Four tasks that make excellent first skills, because they are repeated, opinionated, and currently living only in your head:

Your weekly report The format, the order, the level of detail your manager actually reads. Highest frequency, so highest payoff.
Your team's voice How your company writes: the words you use, the ones you ban, the structure of a good update. Instantly useful to everyone else too.
Notes to decisions Your method for turning a messy transcript into owners, actions and dates. This is judgment, which is exactly what is worth encoding.
Your quality check The pass you personally do before anything ships: what you look for, what you reject, what you send back. The most senior thing you know.
💡 Plan reality. Skills are available on Free, Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise plans, and you upload one under Customize, then Skills, then Add (Anthropic's own instructions). If you cannot upload yet, paste the body of your SKILL.md into a Claude Project as its custom instructions. Same content, same result, one less step of setup. Anthropic also open-sources real examples worth reading before you write yours: github.com/anthropics/skills.
build three

Ship one visible tool

The mechanism here is visibility, not sophistication. When headcount and scope decisions get made, a small tool your team opens every week is undeniable evidence you create leverage. The bar is genuinely "my team opened it twice this week", not production software. Take a spreadsheet your team already touches (campaign tracker, pipeline, budget burn, bug list), paste it into Claude, and run this.

📋 the dashboard prompt 
I'm pasting in a spreadsheet my team uses every week: [say what it is, e.g.
our campaign tracker, our hiring pipeline, our monthly budget burn].

Build me a single-page dashboard as an artifact that my team could open and
actually use. Before you design anything, read the data and tell me the
three questions someone would open this to answer, then build around those
three questions instead of around every column I gave you.

I want a row of the key numbers across the top, two or three charts that
show movement over time rather than a snapshot, and one filter or toggle so
people can narrow it to [the dimension that matters, e.g. channel, owner,
month]. Keep the styling clean and warm, label everything in plain language
rather than column names, and make sure it is readable on a phone.

Flag anything in my data that looks wrong, duplicated or inconsistent
instead of quietly charting it. When it's finished, write me two sentences I
can post in the team channel that say what it answers and who it's for, not
how it was built.
🌱 This is not a stretch, it is what non-engineers at Anthropic do. Their own legal team built a routing prototype, and growth marketing built a workflow that reads CSVs of hundreds of ads and flags the underperformers, plus a plugin that generates up to 100 ad variations in a batch. Their words for the shift: it turns "anyone who can describe a problem into someone who can build a solution" (how Anthropic teams use Claude Code).
💡 If it needs to live at a URL. Artifacts are shareable inside Claude, which is enough for most teams. If people need a permanent link, ask Claude Code to build the same thing as a small static site and deploy it. Same prompt, different output target. Do the artifact version first and only upgrade if people ask for it.
build four

Own the recurring workflow

This is the biggest payoff and the reason it comes last. Pick the recurring deliverable your team quietly dreads, the weekly status report, the campaign recap, the meeting-notes-to-actions pass, and rebuild it as a Claude Project. A Project is a workspace that keeps files and custom instructions attached to every chat inside it, so the context stops being something you re-paste and starts being something the workflow owns. And the workflow runs through you.

  1. 🗂️ Create the Project. In Claude, open Projects and make a new one named after the deliverable. Projects are available on every plan including Free, where you can have up to five (Anthropic's Projects overview).
  2. 📄 Add three finished examples. Three past versions you were genuinely happy with. This is the single highest-leverage step: three real examples teach format and voice better than any amount of describing it.
  3. 📥 Add the raw inputs those examples came from. The messy notes, the exports, the threads. Now Claude can see the whole transformation, input to output, not only the polished end.
  4. ⚙️ Write the project instructions. Use the template below. This is the part most people skip and it is what makes the output consistent instead of merely decent.
  5. 🔁 Run it, then fix the instructions, not the output. When something comes back wrong, resist editing the text by hand. Add a line to the instructions so it never happens again. That is how two hours becomes ten minutes permanently rather than once.
📄 project instructions template 
You produce our [name of the recurring deliverable, e.g. Monday client
status report].

Context. I work as [role] at [type of company or team]. The audience is
[who reads this and what they do with it]. In this project you have [number]
past versions I was happy with, plus the raw inputs each one was built from.
Treat those past versions as the standard for format, structure and voice,
and match them closely rather than improving on them.

Every time I paste raw inputs, do this. Pull out only what the audience will
act on and leave the rest out. Check every number, name and date against the
raw inputs I gave you. Write it in the exact structure of the past versions.
Keep it to [length, e.g. under 300 words or one page]. If something the
audience needs is missing from my inputs, ask me one round of questions
before writing rather than filling the gap yourself.

Never invent a figure, a name or a status. If a claim is not supported by
the inputs, leave it out and list it at the bottom under "needs
confirmation" so I can chase it.

Tone: [how your team actually writes, e.g. plain, direct, no buzzwords, no
hedging].
🌱 Then make it loud, once. Ask Claude to turn the finished Project into a one-page internal playbook: the before and after time, the steps, and how a teammate reuses it. Share that in one meeting. The wage premium for AI skills attaches to people whose work is on the record, and a playbook is on the record in a way a private productivity gain never is.
the honest bit

Where this backfires

AI on the wrong task makes the work worse, and there is a study on it. Harvard Business School and BCG ran a field experiment with 758 consultants at one of the world's top consulting firms. On tasks inside what the researchers named the "jagged technological frontier", the people using AI completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster, and produced results rated over 40% higher in quality. On a task deliberately chosen to sit outside that frontier, the people using AI did worse than the people without it. Same tool, opposite result, and the deciding factor was whether the person knew where the edge was (HBS AI Institute).

Which is the real argument for build one. The audit is not busywork before the fun part, it is how you find your own frontier before you bet a deliverable on it. Practically: run any new workflow twice on work you already know the right answer to, and only then trust it on work you do not. Test before you trust, every time.
