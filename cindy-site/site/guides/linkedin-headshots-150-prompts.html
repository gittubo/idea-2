For a profile picture, start with one of the closer headshots in the bonus library. For posts or a website, try the wider editorial looks. The photos below are AI-generated examples of me; the new prompts are written to work with your own appearance.

Make your first portrait

  1. Pick your tool. Any tool that takes your own photos plus a written prompt will work. The six below are the ones worth your money, and one of them is free to try.
  2. Choose how to supply your face. Either train a likeness on a set of your photos, or attach reference photos to every generation. The trained route looks more like you, the reference route is faster.
  3. Pick one prompt. Copy the complete text, then change clothing or styling details you want to personalise. Keep your actual facial features, age, skin tone and hair consistent with your references.
  4. Generate a small test first. Inspect the likeness, eyes, glasses and skin before creating more variations. Save the strongest image at its original resolution, then make the crop you need.

Route 1: train a likeness

You upload a set of photos once, the tool learns your face, and every image after that is generated from that trained likeness. Most tools ask for 8 to 20 clear photos: different angles, different expressions, no sunglasses, no group shots, nothing heavily filtered. Training usually takes minutes. This is the route that holds your face across dozens of looks, so use it when you want a full set of headshots.

Route 2: attach reference photos

You skip training and attach two or three clear photos of yourself to each generation, alongside the prompt. It is instant and often free, and it gets you close, though the likeness drifts more between images. Use the same reference photos for every variation, so you are judging the styling rather than a different face each time. If you also attach a style reference, keep it for the outfit, lighting or composition only, and let your own photos carry your identity.

Get a portrait that looks like you

A good reference set does more for likeness than adding “photorealistic” ten times. Use photos where your eyes, hairline and facial proportions are easy to see. If a prompt describes a different age, hairstyle or complexion, replace that detail before generating.

For LinkedIn’s profile circle, choose a head-and-shoulders composition with room around the hair and shoulders. Preview it small: your face should still be readable. Keep a full-size original and make a separate crop. The wider studio and office portraits work better in posts, a website biography or personal-brand content.

Make one change at a time

If the face drifts, return to your clearest reference photos and simplify the styling. Compare the eyes, nose, mouth, jaw and hairline. A portrait can be beautifully lit and still need another pass on likeness.

If the skin looks too smooth, request visible natural skin texture and restrained retouching. For warped glasses, use a straight-on reference wearing a similar frame. If the hands distract, pick a closer crop. If the image invents lettering or logos, ask for a plain background and unbranded clothing.

For a useful mini-set, keep your identity references and outfit fixed, then try three directions: a straight-on headshot, a slight shoulder turn and a wider portrait. That gives you options for a profile, a bio and a post without changing everything at once.
